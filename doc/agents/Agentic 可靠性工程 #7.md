The chapters in Part II have built three workflows on the same architecture. Incident response handles failure after it appears. Delivery handles change before it ships. Chaos engineering produces evidence about fragility before it manifests. The remaining workflow is the one that ties them together at the temporal layer: the workflow that operates on signals already in the system, looking forward in time, with the goal of anticipating failure rather than reacting to it. This chapter closes Part II by installing that workflow.

The architectural shift worth naming upfront is small. We have spent four chapters building agents that read signals and act on them. This chapter installs the discipline of reading the same signals and acting on them earlier in their trajectory, before the SLO has broken, before the page has fired, before the team has noticed anything is wrong. The signals do not change. The architecture does not change. What changes is how far ahead the agents are looking and what they are authorised to do when they see something concerning twelve minutes out. The four workflows in Part II are the same architecture pointed at four different temporal positions: pre-change (delivery), post-failure (incident), pre-failure (this chapter), and continuous-discovery (chaos). The architecture is what compounds; the workflow is the operational concern it serves.

What this chapter installs in concept-count is small but consequential. The Context Enrichment Layer (CEL) gets its formal introduction as the architectural primitive that sits between the Signal Plane and the Reasoning Plane. The Capacity Agent earns its full role after its cameo in Chapter 5. The Predictive Reliability Signal (PRS) is the chapter’s headline artefact, the structured assertion the rest of the chapter’s machinery is designed to produce and act on. Three new pieces, all of them serving the same shift: from observability-as-reporting to observability-as-decision-substrate. By chapter close, Part II will be complete.

8.1 From Raw Signals to Situational Awareness
Chapter 5.1 introduced situational awareness as the assembled object the Observability Agent and Topology Agent produce during an incident: the structured artefact that contains the signal, its baseline, its topology context, its change context, its ownership metadata, all with confidence bands attached. The same machinery operates here, and the substance does not need re-deriving. What changes in the pre-incident context is when the assembly happens. During an incident, situational awareness is built reactively, in seconds, from the moment a signal crosses its significance threshold. Pre-incident, situational awareness is maintained continuously, refreshed as evidence accumulates, with the same agents producing the same structured object on a rolling basis whether anything is degrading or not. The artefact that was a response in Chapter 5 becomes a baseline in Chapter 10.

The framing worth installing is that situational awareness is one of the architectural primitives that earns its keep across multiple workflows. Built once, in service of incident response, it now serves pre-incident detection without additional engineering investment. The Topology Agent’s graph is the same graph; the Observability Agent’s signal contracts are the same contracts; the Release Agent’s change events are the same events. Pre-incident intelligence is what happens when you read those artefacts forward in time rather than at the moment of crisis. This is one of the places where the architecture’s coherence pays back the up-front investment most directly. A team that built situational awareness for incident response gets pre-incident detection at the marginal cost of new agents reading the existing substrate.

The operational tempo also changes. Incident-time situational awareness has to assemble in seconds because the cost of waiting is paid in user impact. Pre-incident situational awareness assembles continuously at the rate the signals themselves update, which is typically slower than the human attention cycle and faster than the incident-time response cycle. The architecture can afford to maintain richer context pre-incident because nothing is on fire; the cost of computation is paid in steady state, against signals that update at their own pace, and the resulting context is available the instant the Reasoning Plane needs it. The relationship between pre-incident and incident-time situational awareness is symbiotic: the continuous pre-incident object is what the incident-time machinery reads from when a signal does cross the threshold, which is part of why incident response can converge in seconds rather than minutes at L3 maturity and above.

8.2 Pre-Incident Controls and Intelligent Guardrails
Once situational awareness exists continuously, intervention no longer has to wait for failure. This is the first major architectural shift in this chapter, and it deserves to be named precisely. A pre-incident control is an intervention the architecture takes before a failure surfaces, on the basis of accumulated signal evidence that suggests a failure is likely. Pre-incident controls are not alerts; alerts ask humans to act on the system’s behalf. Pre-incident controls are actions the architecture takes itself, within its authority, against signals that warrant intervention but not escalation. They are the architectural equivalent of an experienced engineer who tightens a rate limit at 14:00 because they have a hunch about what 14:30 is going to look like, except the architecture’s hunch is structured, evidence-backed, and reproducible.

Most teams already have the underlying mechanisms in place. Autoscaling, rate limiting, circuit breaking, service isolation, traffic shaping: these are familiar SRE primitives, present in every modern infrastructure. What changes in agentic pre-incident control is not the mechanism. It is how the mechanism is triggered, how it is governed, and how it is coordinated with other controls. Traditional autoscaling reacts to CPU; agentic autoscaling reacts to projected SLO risk. Traditional rate limiting reacts to volume; agentic rate limiting reacts to upstream signal patterns combined with downstream criticality. Traditional circuit breaking reacts to error thresholds; agentic circuit breaking reacts to evidence that the underlying dependency is degrading even if its surface metrics still look healthy. The primitives are the same. The decisions about when and how to fire them are the change.

The architectural shape worth naming is the Pre-Incident Control Plane. It is not a new plane in addition to the four from Chapter 4.2; it is a particular mode of the Governance Plane operating on input from the Reasoning Plane that itself is operating on continuous situational awareness rather than incident-triggered context. The Pre-Incident Control Plane evaluates a small set of inputs continuously: current SLO health and its trajectory, confidence in the current signal quality, proximity to projected SLO breach, recent change activity, dependency saturation, blast-radius estimate. From these inputs it produces one of three outputs: no action (the system is operating within bounds, no intervention warranted), bounded action (the architecture takes a reversible intervention within its authority, recording the rationale), or escalation (the situation exceeds the architecture’s confidence or authority bounds, the human is paged with context). Most of the time the output is no-action. The valuable cases are the bounded-action ones, where intervention closes the gap before it becomes a page.

To make this concrete against the canonical pre-incident scenario this chapter follows: the payment-service from Chapters 5–7, but now we are watching it at 14:18 on a Tuesday. The Observability Agent’s latency signal is currently nominal (p99 at 215ms, well within the 300ms target). But the situational-awareness object also carries trajectory information. Connection-pool saturation on the shared database has been creeping upward over the last 35 minutes; not enough to alert, but the trend is monotonic. The Release Agent reports a configuration push from 47 minutes ago that adjusted retry behaviour on a downstream service in ways the Reasoning Plane has flagged as a candidate cause. The Topology Agent confirms the connection-pool feeds three tier-1 services. The Pre-Incident Control Plane evaluates: trajectory is concerning, confidence is moderate (0.71), projected SLO breach is roughly 12 minutes out if the current trend continues. The bounded action it takes is to tighten the rate limit on non-critical retry traffic by 30%, scoped to the affected services, time-boxed to 15 minutes, with automatic reversal if the trend stabilises. The Capacity Agent (introduced in 8.4+8.5) is invoked to pre-position additional capacity in case the trend continues. Nothing user-visible has happened. No page has fired. No incident has been declared. The architecture is operating on signals that twelve minutes earlier are still well within nominal bounds, and it is operating on them because the continuous situational-awareness machinery from 8.1 is feeding the Pre-Incident Control Plane evidence the team’s policy considers actionable.

Note
A pre-incident control is the only kind of “alert” that does not ask a human to act. Traditional alerts are messages: the system tells the human something is happening and asks for response. Pre-incident controls are interventions: the system acts on what it sees, records what it did and why, and informs the human as audit rather than ask. The on-call engineer wakes up to read the audit trail, not to make the decision. This is the operational difference that makes anticipation scale: humans cannot evaluate every 12-minute-out projection in real time, and the architecture does not need them to.

The discipline worth installing is that pre-incident controls are always bounded. Every intervention declares its scope, its duration, its reversal path, and its success criteria in the same action-contract format from Chapter 4.5. The architecture does not get to extend the intervention without re-evaluating against policy. The intervention reverses automatically when its success criteria are met or its time-box elapses, whichever comes first. This is the same architectural pattern as autonomous incident execution (Chapter 5.6) and bounded chaos (Chapter 7.6), applied to a different temporal context. The intervention’s blast radius is small; the intervention’s duration is short; the intervention’s reversibility is mandatory. A pre-incident control that cannot be safely reversed is a pre-incident control that should not have been taken; the architecture refuses to authorise it regardless of the Reasoning Plane’s confidence.

The economics worth flagging are that the cost of a false-positive pre-incident control is bounded by its own scope, while the cost of a missed pre-incident opportunity is bounded by user impact. The asymmetry is what makes the discipline viable. A pre-emptive rate limit that turned out not to be needed costs a few minutes of slightly throttled non-critical traffic; a pre-incident opportunity missed costs whatever the incident costs. Most teams find, once they have run the architecture for a quarter, that the false-positive rate is well within their tolerance because the bounded nature of the action keeps the cost manageable, while the catch-rate on actual pre-incident situations is the metric that justifies the investment. The economics tilt the architecture toward acting on moderate-confidence projections, because the downside is small and the upside is incident avoidance. This is the operational basis for the maturity model from Chapter 4.9 placing pre-incident action firmly at L3 and above: the substrate has to be in place before the economics work out, but once they do, the architecture has a structural reason to favour intervention over hesitation.

8.3 From Signals to Context
Pre-incident controls only work if the architecture has enough information to reason about projected risk, not just current state. This is where the difference between signals and context becomes operationally significant, and where the Context Enrichment Layer (CEL) gets its formal introduction. The CEL is the architectural primitive that sits between the Signal Plane and the Reasoning Plane, transforming raw signals into the enriched, structured context the Reasoning Plane needs to make decisions about the future rather than just the present. Chapter 11 will treat the CEL as one of the load-bearing components of the reasoning control plane; this section installs the concept in its operational home.

The conceptual move is subtle but important. Signals are facts about the system: this metric is at this value, this log line was emitted at this time, this trace shows this latency. Context is facts about the system and the situation: this metric is at this value and it has been rising monotonically for 35 minutes against a baseline that itself shifted yesterday; this log line is the seventh of its kind in the last hour from a service whose dependency was just patched; this trace shows this latency which matches the signature of a known fragility pattern the Learning Agent flagged last quarter. The difference is that context carries the temporal, topological, and historical structure the Reasoning Plane needs to project forward in time. Signals alone tell you what is true now. Context tells you what is changing, in what direction, against what baseline, with what corroboration, in what neighbourhood. The CEL is the layer that produces context from signals.

The architectural shape of the CEL has three responsibilities. Enrichment: every signal that enters the CEL is augmented with its baseline (what does “normal” look like for this signal in this service at this time of day), its trajectory (what is this signal’s rate of change), its topology context (what depends on this signal-emitting component), and its change context (what has been deployed or modified in this neighbourhood recently). Correlation: signals that arrive separately but pertain to the same situation are clustered, so the Reasoning Plane reads a coherent picture rather than disconnected events. Projection: where the underlying signal supports it, the CEL emits short-horizon projections (what is this signal likely to be five minutes from now, ten minutes from now, fifteen minutes from now, with what confidence band). The Reasoning Plane consumes the enriched, correlated, projected context; it does not consume raw signals directly. This separation of concerns is what allows the Reasoning Plane to focus on decisions rather than on signal preprocessing.

The CEL is also where one of the architecture’s most important properties lives: grounding. Every piece of context the CEL emits is traceable back to the underlying signals that produced it. When the Reasoning Plane generates a hypothesis, the contributing context elements are attached, and from those, the original signals are reachable. A team auditing a pre-incident control intervention can walk from the action all the way back to the raw signals that made the case for it. Chapter 9 will develop Grounding Rate (GR) as a formal SLO; for now, what matters is that the CEL’s structural commitment to traceability is what makes the rest of the chapter’s machinery defensible. An ungrounded pre-incident intervention is an intervention nobody can justify after the fact, and the architecture’s audit posture cannot tolerate it.

The CEL is not a new system the team has to build. It is a layer assembled from primitives the earlier chapters have already installed: the Observability Agent’s signal contracts (Chapter 2), the Topology Agent’s dependency graph (Chapter 2.4), the Release Agent’s change-event stream (Chapter 6), and the Learning Agent’s similarity store (Chapter 5.8). The CEL is the architectural primitive that combines these into a single enriched context object the Reasoning Plane can consume. A team that has built the Part II substrate has already done most of the engineering work for the CEL; the remaining work is the orchestration that turns existing primitives into a coherent context-production pipeline.

The reason Chapter 11 will return to the CEL is that it becomes the load-bearing component of what Chapter 11.1 calls the reasoning control plane: the layer that determines what context the Reasoning Plane sees, in what order, with what weighting. Note
Signals are facts about the system. Context is facts about the system and the situation. The distinction is not academic; it is what allows the Reasoning Plane to project forward in time rather than just describe the present. A team that operates on signals alone can react quickly; a team that operates on context can intervene early. The CEL is the architectural primitive that makes the second mode operationally tractable.

8.4 + 8.5 Pre-Incident Action: Scaling, Isolation, Migration
When the CEL surfaces context that the Reasoning Plane recognises as a pre-incident risk pattern, the Pre-Incident Control Plane has three families of action available. Scaling preserves SLOs by adding capacity ahead of a projected breach. Isolation preserves SLOs by reducing the load on a degrading component before the degradation becomes user-visible. Migration preserves SLOs by moving work away from an environment whose conditions are deteriorating. The three families are not alternatives; they are different tools for different shapes of pre-incident risk, and the Capacity Agent (full debut in this section) is the role inside the Reasoning Plane that orchestrates among them.

The Capacity Agent first appeared as a cameo in Chapter 5 (the L5 scenario in 5.10, where the architecture caught a failure mode at the canary stage by pre-positioning capacity). Here it earns its full role. The Capacity Agent maintains a continuous model of the system’s capacity envelope across multiple dimensions: compute, connection-pool capacity, queue headroom, network bandwidth, dependency throughput. It reads the CEL’s enriched context to project where the envelope is most likely to be breached, and it proposes capacity actions to the Governance Plane when projected breach probability and time-to-breach cross policy thresholds. Like the other agents in the Reasoning Plane, it does not act unilaterally; it proposes, the Governance Plane authorises, the Execution Plane runs the action under bounded scope.

The three action families have distinct operational shapes. Scaling: agentic autoscaling is not utilisation-driven. It is driven by projected SLO risk. The Capacity Agent watches connection-pool saturation, queue depth, latency distribution, and dependency headroom. When the projected SLO breach probability crosses a threshold, the agent proposes a scale-out, but only if scaling actually helps. The discipline worth naming is restraint: the agent must be able to refuse to scale when scaling would amplify the problem. If the latency rise is caused by downstream database saturation, adding more application replicas increases connection pressure and accelerates failure. The Capacity Agent must detect this case and propose a different intervention (rate limiting non-critical traffic, isolating optional features) rather than reaching for the most obvious lever. Isolation: graduated, not binary. The first step is load shaping (reducing traffic from non-critical callers, throttling best-effort paths). If risk continues to rise, the agent escalates to dependency isolation (cutting a degraded dependency from the request path, forcing fallback behaviour). The escalation is always reversible and always bounded by the team’s policy. Migration: the action of last resort before user impact. Used when the agent determines that risk is environmental rather than logical (the service is healthy but its environment is deteriorating). The Capacity Agent never invents new placements; it selects from pre-approved patterns the team has authorised, with each pattern’s policy declaring how much traffic can be shifted under what conditions to what alternative environment.

The architectural property worth flagging is that pre-incident action graduates. The Capacity Agent rarely fires only one intervention; it queues a sequence of escalating actions, each scoped to a different combination of risk-level and confidence-band, and the architecture fires only the ones the situation actually requires. If the primary action stabilises the signal, the secondary and tertiary are cancelled before they ever run. If the primary is insufficient, the secondary fires, and the architecture re-evaluates whether to continue down the sequence. This is the same pattern as the candidate action set from Chapter 5.5, but applied to a temporal context where the cost of waiting is bounded by the projected time-to-breach rather than by ongoing user impact. The graduation lets the architecture be both aggressive (it intervenes earlier than humans would) and restrained (it does not over-intervene when the simplest action suffices).

The evaluation tempo distinguishes pre-incident graduation from incident-time graduation. In incident response, the architecture has seconds to evaluate each step before the next intervention is needed; the cost of waiting is paid continuously in user impact. In pre-incident action, the architecture has minutes between graduation steps because the user impact has not yet started; the cost of waiting is paid only if the projected breach actually materialises. This means the architecture can afford to wait and observe the effect of each intervention before escalating to the next, which produces more accurate calibration data for the Learning Agent and reduces the rate of over-intervention. The slower tempo is itself an architectural advantage of pre-incident over incident-time response: the architecture gets to operate with more deliberation, which translates to better-calibrated decisions over time.

8.6 AI-Drafted RCAs as a Reliability Control
When something does go wrong despite the pre-incident machinery, the architecture still produces a structured incident record, and the discipline of writing that record changes meaningfully in agentic systems. Traditional root-cause analysis is a document: a human writes prose, attaches diagrams, distributes the artefact, and the team reads it in a meeting next week. Agentic RCAs are the same content with a fundamentally different operational shape. They are structured artefacts the system itself can query, machine-readable, attached to the Learning Agent’s similarity store, and consulted by the Reasoning Plane when future situations resemble the past one. The reframe matters because it determines whether the RCA contributes to compounding reliability or remains a paper artefact that gets filed and forgotten.

The structural shift is that an AI-drafted RCA is not the output of incident learning; it is one input among several to the Closed Learning Loop (Chapter 5.8). The Learning Agent has already captured the incident’s structured data: the candidate actions evaluated, the chosen action, the outcome, the calibration of confidence against observation. The RCA’s job is to add the human-readable narrative layer to that structured record, in a form that both humans and the Reasoning Plane can consult later. The narrative is generated from the structured data, reviewed by the team, and stored as a versioned artefact alongside the structured record. When a future situation triggers similarity-search against the Learning Agent’s case base, the relevant RCAs surface as context the Reasoning Plane can read, not as documents the on-call engineer has to find in a wiki.

The architectural consequence is that RCAs are themselves queryable. The Reasoning Plane consulting an RCA is not reading the prose to summarise it; it is reading the structured fields (the leading hypothesis, the chosen action, the outcome, the contributing factors) and using them as evidence in current reasoning. The human-readable narrative remains attached for review, audit, and the postmortem-review meeting the team still holds, but the operational consumer of the RCA is the architecture itself. This is what we mean when we say an RCA is a control rather than a document. A document sits in a wiki and waits for someone to read it. A control runs continuously, contributing evidence to current decisions, and its value is measured by how often it materially changes what the architecture does.

The structured fields the architecture cares about most are four. Incident signature: the canonical pattern (what changed, what degraded, what cascaded), normalised so that similarity search can find related incidents reliably. Causal chain: the structured sequence from initial trigger to user impact, with each step’s evidence attached. Action ledger: every intervention the architecture took during the incident, with its scope, its effect, its reversal status, and the confidence with which it was authorised. Lesson: the specific update to the architecture’s models that this incident justifies, expressed as a proposed change to a CRS weight, a guardrail threshold, a PRS calibration, or a hypothesis-mining priority. The Learning Agent absorbs the lesson into the relevant model, and the next time a similar pattern appears, the architecture’s response is informed by the lesson the past incident provided.

Note
An RCA the system cannot query is a document. An RCA it can query is a control. The difference is not in the writing; it is in the structure underneath the writing and in whether the architecture can use the structured part as evidence during future reasoning. A team that writes excellent prose-only RCAs and stores them in a wiki has produced a body of human-readable institutional memory and zero machine-readable institutional memory. The compounding curve runs on the second kind, not the first.

The discipline worth flagging is that the AI-drafted part is not the headline. The Learning Agent’s structured capture is the load-bearing artefact; the AI-drafted narrative is the layer of human-readable text that makes the artefact accessible to humans without compromising its machine-readability. The temptation to dwell on AI-drafted narratives as an end in themselves is the same temptation Chapter 5 named with respect to dashboards: the artefact looks impressive, but its operational value depends on whether it contributes to the rest of the architecture. An RCA whose narrative reads well but whose structured fields are sparse or wrong is an RCA that fails its operational role even if it satisfies the postmortem-review audience.

The temporal property worth noting is that the RCA-as-control comes alive most visibly in the pre-incident context. When the PRS fires on a pattern that resembles a past incident, the architecture reaches into the Learning Agent’s similarity store, retrieves the relevant RCAs, and reads their structured fields as part of the context the Reasoning Plane is operating on. The PRS’s contributing-signals field carries references to the past RCAs that made the current pattern recognisable. The Pre-Incident Control Plane’s policy decision can cite the past outcome as part of its rationale. Past incidents become current evidence, and the system’s response is informed by what it has previously learned. This is the loop that closes Part II’s compounding-curve thesis: each incident produces structured evidence that future pre-incident detection consumes, and the architecture’s anticipation sharpens as the case base grows.

8.7 Predictive Reliability Signal: Predicting Risk Before Failure
We come to the chapter’s headline artefact. Predictive Reliability Signal (PRS) is the structured output the Reasoning Plane produces when it recognises that the system is drifting toward conditions historically associated with SLO breach. PRS is not a prediction in the meteorological sense, not a forecast of failure with a probability. It is a structured assertion: based on the current Context Enrichment Layer state, on the Learning Agent’s similarity store, and on the Capacity Agent’s projection models, the system has crossed into a state where the probability of failure within a defined horizon exceeds the policy’s actionable threshold. The PRS is what the Pre-Incident Control Plane reads to decide whether to act, and it is the artefact this chapter’s machinery is designed to produce.

The conceptual move worth slowing down on is that the PRS is not an alert. Alerts are events triggered by threshold crossings; a metric exceeded its line, an event fired, a human was paged. The PRS is a continuous structured assessment, refreshed every few seconds, with the form of a confidence-bearing claim: this region of the system has elevated breach risk because of these signals, with this confidence, against this historical reference, on this time horizon. The PRS feeds policy; the policy decides whether to act, escalate, or observe. The PRS is to pre-incident reliability what the candidate action set is to incident response: the structured artefact the rest of the machinery operates on.

A PRS has five fields. Risk region: which services or components are at elevated risk. Risk type: what kind of failure the agent projects (saturation, dependency degradation, change-induced regression, environmental drift). Confidence: how strongly the Reasoning Plane believes this projection, calibrated by the Learning Agent’s past accuracy on similar projections. Horizon: how far out in time the projected breach is (seconds, minutes, occasionally tens of minutes for slow-burn patterns). Contributing signals: the structured evidence the projection rests on, traceable back to the underlying telemetry through the CEL’s grounding chain. The five fields together let the Pre-Incident Control Plane evaluate every PRS against policy: a high-confidence PRS with a short horizon and severe risk type may trigger autonomous action; a moderate-confidence PRS with a long horizon may trigger elevated readiness without intervention; a low-confidence PRS with conflicting signals may trigger only increased sampling and observation.

The lifecycle of a PRS has four phases worth naming. Emergence: the Reasoning Plane recognises that the current CEL context matches a pattern the Learning Agent’s similarity store associates with elevated risk, and emits an initial PRS with low-to-moderate confidence and a wider horizon band. Strengthening: as additional signals corroborate the pattern, the PRS’s confidence rises and its horizon estimate tightens. The Pre-Incident Control Plane re-evaluates the PRS against policy at each refresh, and the architecture’s response level may shift as confidence crosses thresholds. Action: when the PRS crosses the policy’s actionable threshold, the Pre-Incident Control Plane invokes the appropriate agent (typically the Capacity Agent) to propose interventions. The PRS remains active during the intervention, and the Reasoning Plane continues to monitor whether the intervention is reducing the projected risk. Resolution: the PRS resolves in one of three ways. The intervention succeeds, the trajectory stabilises, the confidence drops, and the PRS becomes inactive. The intervention fails, the trajectory continues, and the situation escalates to incident response (Chapter 5). The signal pattern was a false positive, the projected breach does not materialise, and the PRS resolves with the Learning Agent recording a calibration miss the model will use to sharpen future projections.

8.8 Automated Prevention: Turning Anticipation into Safe Action
A PRS is a signal. Turning it into action is the work this section addresses. The good news is that most of the machinery is already in place from earlier chapters. The candidate action set framework from Chapter 5.5 gives the Reasoning Plane its structured shape for proposing actions. The action-contract format from Chapter 4.5 gives every action its scope, reversibility, and success criteria. The Governance Plane’s authority tiers from Chapter 5.6 govern what may execute autonomously versus what must escalate. What changes in the pre-incident context is the stakes calculus: the action is being taken before any user has been affected, on the basis of projected rather than observed failure, with the cost of being wrong distributed differently than during an incident.

The two failure modes worth flagging are different from the incident-response ones. The first is over-intervention: the architecture takes pre-incident action on a PRS that turns out to be a false positive, and the intervention itself causes a problem. A pre-emptive scale that triggers a cold-start cascade. A rate limit that throttles legitimate traffic. A migration that fails to converge. All of these are worse than no action, because the intervention’s cost is paid in user impact even though the predicted failure never materialises. The second is under-intervention: the architecture observes the PRS, decides not to act because confidence is moderate or horizon is long, and the predicted failure happens anyway. The cost here is paid in the same way as if the architecture had no pre-incident capability at all, but with the added insult that the system saw it coming and chose not to act. Both failure modes are calibration problems. The Learning Agent’s job is to keep both costs in balance over time; the Calibration Error SLO from Chapter 3.6 is the canary that flags when the balance has shifted.

The discipline that distinguishes safe pre-incident action from gambling on predictions is reversibility. Every pre-incident intervention must be reversible automatically, within bounded time, without user impact from the reversal itself. A pre-emptive scale that adds capacity reverses by removing capacity once the projected risk subsides. A rate limit that throttles non-critical traffic reverses by removing the throttle. An isolation that disables an optional feature reverses by re-enabling it. The action-contract format requires the reversal path to be declared at the time the action is proposed, and the architecture enforces the reversal whether or not anyone remembers it. This is the architectural defence against the “I forgot to undo the temporary fix” failure mode that haunts manual operations. The Capacity Agent does not forget; the architecture’s audit trail records the action and its scheduled reversal, and the reversal fires when the conditions are met.

Warning
Pre-emptive action without a reversal path is gambling on the prediction. If the prediction is correct, the action helps; if the prediction is wrong, the action becomes a problem that someone has to undo manually. Manual undoing is exactly the kind of toil agentic reliability is supposed to eliminate, and a pre-incident control that leaves manual undoing in its wake is a control that does not earn its keep. Reversibility is not optional. It is the architectural property that makes pre-incident action safe enough to scale.

The other property worth flagging is proportionality. The Capacity Agent’s graduated action sequence from 8.4+8.5 is the architectural form of proportionality: the smallest sufficient intervention fires first, and the architecture escalates only if the smaller intervention proves insufficient. This is the same pattern as graduated incident response from Chapter 5.6 and graduated chaos from Chapter 7.6. Proportionality is what allows pre-incident action to be both aggressive (it intervenes earlier than humans would) and humble (it does not commit the architecture to large interventions on moderate signals). The architecture earns the right to take larger interventions only when smaller ones have proven insufficient and the PRS’s confidence has risen accordingly. The graduation is itself a form of evidence-gathering: each step in the sequence produces signal about whether the underlying hypothesis is correct, and the architecture updates its confidence as the evidence arrives.

The human’s role here is the same shape as in delivery and incident response: above the loop most of the time, in the loop only for the cases the policy routes to them. The above-the-loop work is the design of the pre-incident policy: which PRS shapes warrant which action classes, what the confidence and horizon thresholds are for autonomous action, which interventions remain human-approved regardless of confidence. The in-the-loop work is the rare case where a PRS surfaces a structurally novel pattern, or where the proposed intervention would exceed the architecture’s autonomous authority, or where multiple agents disagree about the right response. The on-the-loop work is the steady-state monitoring of pre-incident outcomes: did the architecture’s PRSs prove correct over the last month, did the interventions reduce projected risk in the way the model predicted, is the Calibration Error trending in the direction the policy expects. The shift from incident-time human attention to pre-incident policy attention is one of the operationally most consequential shifts the architecture produces. The same human who used to be paged at 4am for a payment-service latency incident is now reviewing PRS calibration trends in a planning meeting at 11am the following month. The work is no less important. It is differently shaped, and it scales.

8.9 Coordinating Preventive Actions: Control Orchestration
As the agent ecosystem grows, a new risk surface appears that the earlier chapters of Part II only gestured at. The Capacity Agent proposes a scale-out. The Test Agent flags a validation concern with the change-control state. The Chaos Agent reports an ongoing experiment in the same region. The Release Agent reports a deploy in flight. All four agents are correct in isolation, and all four are operating on the same physical system. Without coordination, they can produce conflicting interventions: the Capacity Agent scales out while the Chaos Agent is injecting controlled latency, and the combined effect is not what either intended. An unorchestrated agent ecosystem is multiple agents disagreeing in parallel, and the larger the ecosystem grows, the more often this disagreement produces emergent failure of its own.

This section foreshadows the SRE Orchestrator, which Chapter 14.2 develops formally as the architectural component that resolves these conflicts. The Orchestrator is not another reasoning agent; it is the policy-enforcement layer that mediates between agents whose actions may interact. When two agents propose actions that conflict, the Orchestrator evaluates the proposals against the policy that declares which agent’s authority prevails under which conditions. When two agents propose actions that compose without conflict, the Orchestrator passes both through. When the situation is novel enough that the policy does not cover it, the Orchestrator escalates to a human rather than letting any single agent break the tie unilaterally. The Orchestrator is what allows the agent ecosystem to scale without becoming an internal coordination problem worse than the external reliability problem it was meant to solve.

For the pre-incident context specifically, the Orchestrator’s most common decisions involve three patterns. Prevention versus delivery: when a deploy is in flight and a PRS fires, the Orchestrator decides whether the pre-incident action should pause, modify, or proceed alongside the deploy. Prevention versus chaos: when a chaos experiment is active and a PRS fires in the same region, the Orchestrator decides whether the experiment should abort (the production signal is now more important than the experimental one) or continue (the experiment is the cause and aborting it will resolve the PRS). Prevention versus prevention: when two pre-incident actions are proposed that would interact, the Orchestrator decides which fires, which is queued, and which is rejected. None of this is complicated in concept; all of it is structurally necessary for an agent ecosystem operating at L4 or above.

The property worth flagging before the formal treatment in Chapter 14.2 is that the Orchestrator’s authority is derived from policy, not from its own reasoning. The Orchestrator does not decide which agent’s proposal is better; it applies the policy the team has written, which declares which agent’s proposal prevails under which conditions. When the policy does not cover the current situation, the Orchestrator does not improvise; it escalates to a human. This is the same authority discipline that protects every other component in the architecture: agents propose, policy decides, the Governance Plane enforces, humans handle the cases policy did not anticipate. The Orchestrator is the architectural component that makes this discipline scale across an agent ecosystem rather than just a single agent’s actions.

Note
An unorchestrated agent ecosystem is multiple agents disagreeing in parallel. Each agent is locally correct; the system as a whole is incoherent. The architectural answer is the SRE Orchestrator (Chapter 14.2), but the discipline starts here: agents must propose actions to a layer that can see all the other proposals, and the architecture must be able to refuse compositions that conflict regardless of any single agent’s confidence. The L3-to-L4 maturity transition depends on this property being in place; L5 cannot be reached without it.

The architectural property to flag before moving to 8.10 is that the Orchestrator’s existence is what allows pre-incident intelligence to scale. With one or two agents in the Reasoning Plane, coordination can be handled by the Governance Plane directly. With five or six agents (the current cast of Part II) the coordination problem becomes structural, and a dedicated orchestration component becomes necessary. Chapter 14 will develop the SRE Orchestrator in operational detail. For now, it is enough to flag that the architecture’s growth from “Reasoning Plane with one agent” through “Reasoning Plane with the recurring cast” requires a coordination primitive the earlier chapters did not need. Pre-incident intelligence is where the requirement first becomes concrete.

8.10 Observability as a Decision Substrate
Chapter 10 closes Part II. The reader has now seen the same architecture point at four different workflows: incident response (Chapter 5), delivery (Chapter 6), chaos engineering (Chapter 7), and pre-incident intelligence (this chapter). Each workflow has its own operational shape, its own recurring concerns, its own characteristic failure modes. None of them required a different architecture. The same four planes from Chapter 4, the same recurring agents, the same DRAL loop, the same five flagship SLOs. The architecture is the invariant; the workflows are the local applications.

The reframing this chapter completes is that observability is no longer a support function. Observability used to be the thing that told humans when something was wrong, in time for humans to react. Across Part II, observability has been reshaped into something different: a decision substrate that the architecture’s reasoning operates on continuously, producing structured assessments of system state that feed bounded autonomous action across the workflows the team cares about. The dashboards are still there. The pages still fire when they need to. What changed is that the dashboards and pages are no longer the primary consumers of observability signals; the agents are. The architecture reads observability the way a senior engineer reads it: not as a source of alerts but as a source of context for ongoing decisions. The difference is that the architecture reads it continuously, at machine speed, across the whole system, twenty-four hours a day. The team’s senior engineers cannot do that. They were never meant to.

The compounding curve from Chapter 3 lives across all four Part II workflows, and Chapter 10 is where the compounding becomes most visible. Every incident produces RCAs the architecture can query; every delivery produces calibration data the CRS absorbs; every chaos experiment produces fragility evidence the Chaos Agent’s mining models sharpen against; every PRS that fires (correctly or not) produces calibration data the Learning Agent uses to tighten future projections. The four workflows feed each other. An incident in payment-service generates an RCA that lives in the Learning Agent’s store; six weeks later, a PRS firing on a similar pattern reads that RCA and acts earlier than it would have without it. A chaos experiment surfaces a fragility in shared connection pools; eight weeks later, a delivery’s CRS reads that fragility as Historical Volatility and routes the change through a more conservative pattern. None of this is magic. It is the operational consequence of having the same structured artefacts flow through the same architecture across different workflows.

There is a property of the architecture worth naming explicitly before Part II closes. The architecture prefers small actions early to large actions late. Every workflow we have developed in Part II expresses this preference structurally. Delivery’s CRS bands route routine changes through routine patterns and only escalate when the score genuinely warrants escalation. Chaos’s bounded experiments produce evidence in small increments rather than dramatic exercises. Incident response’s authority tiers favour low-blast-radius autonomous actions over high-blast-radius human-approved ones. Pre-incident intelligence’s graduated action sequences fire the smallest sufficient intervention first. The preference is not stylistic. It is the architectural expression of a value: autonomy earns its place by being right about small things often, not by being right about large things rarely. The compounding curve runs on this preference. A team whose architecture defaults to large interventions cannot accumulate the kind of evidence the Learning Agent needs to calibrate against; a team whose architecture defaults to small interventions builds up an immense base of bounded outcomes the architecture can learn from.

The transition the rest of the book makes is to look inside the Reasoning Plane. Until now, this book has treated the Reasoning Plane as a structured black box: it consumes context, produces candidate actions and PRSs, and the rest of the architecture operates on its outputs. Part III opens up what happens inside that box. Decision graphs (Chapter 9). Multi-hypothesis reasoning (Chapter 9). Calibration Error formally defined (Chapter 9). Reasoning patterns and trade-off modelling (Chapter 10). Governance under autonomy and the Decision Budget (Chapter 11). Simulation as the substrate for trust (Chapter 12). Four chapters that show how the agents actually think, and what the architecture has to do to keep that thinking trustworthy as autonomy expands. Part II showed agents acting. Part III shows agents reasoning. The architecture is the same. What we look at changes.
