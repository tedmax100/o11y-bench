# Chapter 2. The Shift to Agentic Engineering

For most of the last decade, Site Reliability Engineering has been framed as a scaling problem with a known solution. Automate more, alert better, document harder, hire smarter engineers. That framing worked, until it stopped working. The problem today is not that systems are unreliable. It is that they have grown beyond what humans can reliably reason about in real time.

Consider a senior on-call engineer at 03:00. A latency alert fires on a checkout path. The runbook calls for a five-step diagnostic. Recent deployments, dependency health, traffic pattern, regional capacity, configuration drift. The engineer is good at it, which is exactly why they are on the rota. They pivot through four dashboards to find recent changes, cross-reference a deployment with a config push, scan a Slack thread for a rumour about a noisy neighbour in another region, and run a topology lookup that takes ninety seconds because the metadata service is in the same region that just degraded. By the time they have a working hypothesis, the blast radius has tripled. Nothing in that sequence was a tooling failure. Every system did its job. The dashboards rendered. The runbook was correct. The Slack thread was honest. The bottleneck was the human assembling context, and the human is now structurally too slow for the speed at which the system needs that context assembled.

This is not a story about that engineer doing anything wrong. The opposite. They did everything the role asks for, and the role asks for the impossible. Multiply that night by a quarter, and you get a team that is exhausted in a way that no number of additional engineers will fix, because the bottleneck is not how many people are on the rota. It is what the rota is asking each of them to do once an alert fires. Hire two more senior engineers and you have two more people each running the same impossible diagnostic at 03:00.

In practice, what we see across mature engineering organisations is not a lack of tooling or process. It is a growing cognitive bottleneck. Engineers are expected to hold entire systems in their heads while responding to incidents, approving changes, interpreting telemetry, and managing risk. Even with excellent observability and well-defined runbooks, the volume and velocity of signals exceed what attention can absorb. The result is familiar. Alert fatigue, slower incident response, conservative change practices, and teams who spend more time preserving reliability than improving it. None of these are personal failures. They are what happens when a role designed around an assumption (that the human stays at the centre of the loop) meets a system that has outgrown that assumption.

The industry has responded to this pressure with automation. Scripts, pipelines, auto-remediation, and more recently AI-assisted tools. Automation helped, but it plateaued. Automation executes predefined steps faster. It does not understand context, reason about trade-offs, or adapt when conditions drift. As systems become more dynamic, automation alone becomes brittle. Every new exception adds another rule. Every new dependency adds another conditional. Over time the automation stack becomes a second system the engineer has to understand during failure, and at scale, that stack does not reduce cognitive load. It shifts it. It scales execution, not judgment.

This is the structural mismatch the rest of this book is about. We have built systems whose state and dynamics now exceed human-paced sense-making, and we have responded by automating more of what humans used to do, without changing the part of the loop that humans were always going to be the bottleneck in. We have, in a real sense, optimised the wrong part of the loop for the last decade. The shift this book argues for is the one that closes the gap. From systems that wait to be told what to do, to systems that observe, reason, and act within boundaries that humans have defined. From an engineering role measured by how quickly the on-call can assemble context at 03:00, to an engineering role measured by how well the system reasons before 03:00 even happens. We call the discipline that emerges from that shift agentic reliability engineering.

1.1 The Unsustainable Scaling Crisis in SRE
Site Reliability Engineering was created to solve a specific problem: how to operate large-scale systems reliably without relying on heroics. For a long time, the answer was clear. Standardise practices, automate repetitive work, measure everything, treat reliability as an engineering problem rather than an operational afterthought. Error budgets gave teams a numerate language for risk. Toil reduction gave them a discipline for spending engineering time well. That model worked when systems scaled linearly and human understanding could still keep pace with system behaviour. My view is that we have now passed that point.

The reliability crisis most organisations experience today is not caused by a lack of maturity. It is caused by scale colliding with human cognition. Systems change faster than people can reason about them. Dependencies multiply faster than teams can map them. Telemetry grows faster than engineers can interpret it. Reliability has become a problem of attention, not intention. Three breakdowns make the gap visible: alerting, incident response, and change management. These are not three separate problems. They are three faces of the same one.

The first is alerting. As systems grow, alerts multiply. Teams respond by tuning, grouping, suppressing, and documenting alerts until alerting itself becomes a full-time job. At that point, alerts stop being signals and start becoming noise-management exercises. Engineers are no longer responding to reality. They are responding to filters they created in order to survive. The team that owns the alerting policy is now running a small internal product whose customers are themselves, and whose product is a tolerable on-call burden. That is a strange thing to be paying senior engineers to build.

Note
Alert fatigue is not a tooling failure. It is a symptom of exceeding human attention capacity. No amount of clever grouping or routing fixes a problem that is, at its root, about the size of the cognitive load the role demands.

The second is incident response. Timelines stretch, not because teams are slow, but because context assembly is slow. Engineers spend critical minutes reconstructing what changed, what is related, and what matters. They pivot between observability tools because no single tool knows the whole system. They piece together a recent deployment, a configuration push, an infra change, and a third-party incident upstream, each captured by a different system, none of which speaks the others’ language fluently. By the time clarity emerges, the system has often already degraded further. The decisions that mattered most needed to be made before the context was available. Which is to say, they could not be made by humans at all under modern conditions, regardless of how good those humans were at their job.

The third is change management. As confidence drops, organisations add gates. Reviews become longer. Approval boards grow. The number of people who need to bless a deployment rises in inverse proportion to anyone’s actual confidence that the deployment is safe. Velocity slows, not because teams lack skill, but because uncertainty increases, and humans respond to uncertainty by adding process. That works, for a while. Then the process itself becomes the bottleneck, and the meeting cadence becomes the constraint on how fast a change can ship. At that point governance has stopped protecting reliability and started taxing it. The only honest accounting is that the gates are buying the appearance of safety, not the underlying property.

In each of these breakdowns the assumption is the same: that a human will sit in the middle of the loop, holding enough context to decide. That assumption was correct for most of SRE’s history. It is no longer correct. The volume of signals, the speed of change, and the depth of dependencies have outpaced what any individual can carry, and conservative process growth around the human has become a substitute for capacity rather than a complement to it. We have, in effect, made the human into the integration layer for systems that have outgrown integration by humans. The result is not just an exhausted on-call rota. It is a structural ceiling on how reliable our systems can become.

This is the gap that Agentic Reliability Engineering, or ARE, is designed to close. ARE is the discipline of building reliability systems that can observe context, reason about trade-offs, and act within boundaries of intent set by humans, so that the human’s role moves above the loop, to design and oversight, rather than into the loop, as the real-time decision engine. We are not replacing SRE. We are evolving it. The error budget does not change. The SLO (service level objective) does not change. What changes is who reads the alert, who scores the options, and who acts inside the boundary the team agreed on. The rest of this book is a tour of what that discipline looks like in practice.

1.2 Defining the Agentic System
Before going further, we need to be precise about language. Many of the problems in this space come from overloaded terms. Automation, intelligence, autonomy, and agency are used interchangeably, even though they describe very different capabilities. Clarity here is not academic. It is foundational, because the design choices we make in later chapters depend on which of these we are actually building, and a team that conflates an LLM-backed assistant with an agentic system will design (and deploy) the wrong artefact.

An agentic system is a system that can perceive its environment, reason about what it observes, and take action within boundaries that are defined by human intent. Three elements, equal weight. Remove any one of them and the system stops being agentic. A system that perceives but does not reason is monitoring. A system that reasons but cannot act is analysis. A system that acts but does not perceive or reason is automation. The agentic system is the one that does all three, inside a bounded envelope of intent. The bounded envelope is doing as much work in that sentence as the three capabilities are.

This is what separates an agentic system from the kind of automation we have lived with for two decades. Automation focuses on execution. It follows predefined instructions when specific conditions are met. If condition A occurs, perform action B. The model is deterministic and predictable, which is exactly why it has been so valuable in operations, and exactly why it becomes brittle as systems become more dynamic. Automation does not understand why it is acting. It does not evaluate alternatives. It does not adapt when conditions drift outside what was anticipated. When something unexpected happens, automation either fails silently, escalates to a human, or does the wrong thing confidently. The confident wrong thing is the failure mode that has produced most of the cautionary tales the SRE community tells about automation in the first place.

Note
Automation executes a step. An agentic system chooses among steps. The difference looks small in the diagram and is the entire point in production.

Agentic systems behave differently. They evaluate context rather than triggers. They assess confidence rather than certainty. They choose actions based on intent and constraints rather than static rules. Importantly, they do not need to be correct all the time. They need to be bounded, observable, and accountable. That is a very different engineering target than “always right”, and it is a target reliability engineers already know how to hit, because it is the same target an error budget hits. We build systems that are good enough most of the time, that surface their uncertainty, and that have an explicit story for what happens when they are wrong. We do not require omniscience. We require honesty about confidence and a designed response to error.

It is worth naming a few of the misinterpretations that appear most often when teams introduce these systems, because the misnaming has practical consequences. Conversational assistants that summarise dashboards or draft incident updates are not agents. They observe and describe, but they do not reason or act, and they sit outside the reliability loop rather than inside it. Rule engines tuned by machine learning are not agents either. The model classifies, but the decisions remain hard-coded, and behaviour becomes brittle the moment conditions drift outside what was anticipated. Reflexive auto-remediation pipelines that fire on a single threshold are not agents. They react, they do not weigh, and they cannot decline to act when declining is the right call. None of these are bad. Most are useful. But they are not agentic, and treating them as if they were is how teams end up surprised by their systems in production.

The human role under this definition is what the book will return to throughout, so we name it once now and use it consistently. We will refer to the human as the above-the-loop human: the engineer, team, or organisation that defines intent, sets constraints, reviews outcomes, and intervenes when the system encounters situations it cannot resolve safely. Above the loop is not absent from the loop. It is positioned where humans add the most value: in judgment, in accountability, and in the design of the system itself. The agent is in the loop because the loop now runs at machine speed. The human is above it because the loop now runs without them, most of the time, by design. The exceptions, when the system surfaces them, are the moments the human’s day actually matters.

A clean working test, then, for the rest of the book. If a system perceives its environment through structured signals, chooses among options against a model of what it should be optimising for, and acts inside an envelope that a human authored, it is agentic. If any of those three properties is missing, it is something else. Naming it something else is not a failure, it is hygiene. Most of the reliability stack today is not agentic, and that is fine. What is not fine is calling it agentic and then deploying it as if it were.

1.3 Why Maturity Determines the Return on Agentic Systems
The promise of agentic systems often sounds universal: faster recovery, smarter decisions, reduced toil, autonomous execution. What is less often said is that these outcomes are not evenly distributed. The same agentic capability can produce radically different results depending on the maturity of the system it is introduced into.

This is not a limitation of AI. It is a property of systems. Agentic systems do not create order; they amplify it. They do not introduce discipline; they accelerate whatever discipline already exists. When foundations are weak, autonomy does not mask the problem. It exposes it earlier and more visibly. When foundations are strong, autonomy compounds their effect. This is why some teams see dramatic gains from early adoption while others experience confusion, instability, and a loss of trust, and why two teams running similar pilots can come away with opposite conclusions about whether the technology works.

In low-maturity environments, humans have been quietly subsidising the system. They cross-check dashboards because they don’t trust any single source. They infer ownership from social knowledge because the tagging is wrong. They tolerate ambiguity because they have to, and they fill in missing context with experience the documentation never captured. Introduce an agent and that subsidy disappears. Conflicting signals become conflicting decisions. Missing metadata becomes invisible relationships. Unclear ownership becomes actions that cannot be responsibly executed. The agent did not break anything. It removed the buffer that was hiding what was already broken. A team that misdiagnoses that moment as an “AI problem” will spend a year tuning the wrong system.

In high-maturity environments the same mechanisms produce the opposite effect. Signals are curated. Telemetry is intentional. Services are tagged, owned, and connected. Change flows are observable and reversible. Here, agents are not guessing. They are reasoning. Decisions are not speculative. They are grounded. Each autonomous decision builds on previous learning. The return is not linear. It compounds.

![image](https://hackmd.io/_uploads/rJGQ8-Tbfe.png)

The practical implication is that ARE is earned in levels. There is a maturity model. We will lay it out formally in Chapter 4.9, and where you sit on it determines what kinds of autonomy will help you, what kinds will hurt you, and what foundations you need to invest in before the next level of autonomy becomes safe. For now the only thing the reader needs to carry forward is this: nobody is rolling out ARE at L5 (level 5 maturity) on day one. The goal of this book is not to convince you to skip the work. It is to show you what the work is, in order, so that the autonomy you deploy makes you stronger rather than faster-at-being-wrong. The first return on agentic adoption, for most teams, is not faster recovery. It is honest visibility into where the existing reliability story was being held together by people the documentation forgot to mention.

1.4 Intent as the Missing Abstraction
Most reliability systems fail in the same way. Not because they lack data, automation, or intelligence, but because they lack clarity of intent.

Traditional SRE practices encode behaviour indirectly. Thresholds imply acceptable risk. Runbooks imply priority. Escalation paths imply ownership. None of these explicitly state what the system is trying to achieve. They describe actions, not purpose. Automation can tolerate that indirection, because when the proxy drifts away from the goal, a human is there to correct the outcome. Autonomy cannot. An agent must choose between actions, weigh trade-offs, and decide when not to act. Those decisions cannot be encoded reliably through thresholds alone. They require an explicit expression of what the system is meant to be optimising for. That expression is what we will call intent.

Intent is a structured declaration of the outcomes the system is trying to produce, the constraints it must respect, and the trade-offs it is permitted to make. It can change by service, by environment, by time, or by context. The same service may prioritise availability over cost during peak hours and efficiency over availability during off-hours, and an agent operating under explicit intent can respect that distinction without anyone writing a special-case runbook. Intent is not a vague mission statement. It is a first-class input to the reliability system, sitting alongside telemetry and policy. The moment it earns that status, much of what is currently hard-coded as “if X then Y” can become “given the current intent, what does Y look like right now.”

The contrast with traditional thresholding is sharp, and worth working through carefully because it is the contrast the rest of the book rests on. Consider two ways of expressing the same operational goal: keeping a checkout service performant during a sale.

The first is a rule: “If CPU on the checkout service exceeds 80% for more than three minutes, scale by two replicas. If memory exceeds 85%, page on-call.” This is what most of our automation looks like today. It is concrete, testable, and easy to write. It is also brittle. It says nothing about what the team actually cares about, which is customer experience. It will scale the service when CPU is high even if latency is fine. It will not scale the service when CPU is fine but latency is degraded by an upstream dependency. It will page on-call at 85% memory even if the service is autoscaling cleanly and no customer has noticed. The rule encodes a proxy for what the team wants, and when the proxy drifts away from the goal, the system optimises for the proxy.

The second is intent: “Keep p99 (99th percentile) checkout latency under 200ms during peak hours. Prefer cost-efficient capacity below that threshold. Do not exceed two replicas without human approval. Do not change capacity at all during the final hour of an active sale window unless latency exceeds 300ms.” This is longer to write, and harder to write well. It is also the difference between a system that scales the right thing for the right reason and a system that follows a rule. An agent operating under that intent can scale on latency rather than CPU, hold capacity steady through the end of a sale to avoid disrupting a stable system, and surface the conflict honestly when the constraints disagree. The team is no longer telling the system what to do. They are telling it what to be true, and trusting it to choose among the actions that make that true.

Tip
A working definition of intent: the goal you would explain to a smart new SRE on their first day, written so a machine can act on it. If you cannot write the explanation, the agent cannot operate on it. If the explanation is not machine-actionable, you have a poster, not a primitive.

Intent has three properties that matter for the rest of the book. It is explicit: written down, versioned, and reviewable, not implicit in someone’s head or hidden in the gaps between three different runbooks. It is bounded: paired with the limits within which an agent is permitted to honour it, so that “prefer cost-efficient capacity” never becomes “scale to zero during a flash crowd” and “minimise customer impact” never becomes “block every change until the next maintenance window.” And it is observable: the system can report which intent it is currently following and why, so that disagreement between an agent’s action and a human’s expectation becomes a constructive conversation rather than an outage post-mortem.

Once intent is the primary abstraction, much else falls into place. Reliability stops being about responding to symptoms and becomes about preserving outcomes. Agents stop optimising for whatever signal is loudest and start optimising for what the organisation actually wants. Disagreement becomes traceable rather than mysterious. The reliability conversation moves from “why did the system do that” to “which intent was it following, and is that intent still right.” That is a more useful conversation. It is the one the rest of this book is designed to support.

1.5 Trust Boundaries and the Limits of Autonomy
Autonomy without limits is not intelligence. It is risk.

One of the most common misconceptions about agentic systems is that success depends on how much control we give up. The opposite is true. Agentic systems work best when their authority is clearly bounded. Trust is not created by removing constraints. It is created by designing them well. In reliability engineering, the deliverable that makes autonomy usable is not the autonomy. It is the boundary the autonomy operates inside. We will call this the autonomy boundary.

The autonomy boundary is the explicit, machine-enforceable specification of what an agent is permitted to do, under what conditions, and how it can be stopped. We will reserve the term trust boundary for the social and contractual aspect of the same idea (what humans, teams, and organisations have agreed the agent may do) and use autonomy boundary for the engineered artefact. The distinction matters because the social agreement and the technical enforcement need to match, and slippage between them is one of the most common ways autonomy gets a team in trouble.

A good autonomy boundary has three properties. It is explicit: the agent’s permitted actions, conditions, and stop mechanisms are written down in a form both humans and the agent can read. It is machine-enforceable: the boundary is not advice, it is code that runs in the path of every decision the agent makes, and a decision outside the boundary does not execute. And it is demotable: when confidence drops, when novel conditions arise, or when a human override is invoked, the agent’s authority can be reduced to a narrower envelope without restarting the system or losing state. A boundary that cannot be tightened in real time is not a boundary. It is a hope.

Warning
Autonomy without a demotable boundary is not autonomy. It is unsupervised execution. Every agent you deploy must be able to be made less autonomous, instantly, without breaking the system around it.

The aviation analogy is the one that survives the most scrutiny here, and I will use it once and stop. Autopilot does not mean the pilot stops flying. It means the pilot’s role becomes one of supervising, configuring, and intervening, and the system is designed so that the pilot can take direct control at any moment, with the airframe still flying in the meantime. Autopilot also has explicit envelopes: altitudes it will not exceed, attitudes it will not adopt, conditions under which it disengages itself and surfaces the controls back to the pilot. That set of properties (supervisable, configurable, interruptible, and self-disengaging at the limit) is what we want our agentic reliability systems to look like. Autonomy is not a position on a dial between zero and one. It is a designed envelope, and the envelope is the deliverable.

A second point follows directly from this. Human override is not a failure of autonomy. It is a feature of it. Agents should expect to be overridden. They should surface their reasoning when they are. They should log which intent they were following and which boundary they were honouring, so that the override becomes a structured signal rather than a one-line incident note. When the override is a signal that the intent was wrong, the team adjusts the intent. When the override is a signal that the boundary was too loose, the team tightens the boundary. When the override is a signal that the agent was right and the human was wrong (which happens, and which the system should be honest about), the team learns something else again. None of this is possible if override is treated as evidence the system failed. Removing the ability to override does not make a system safer; it makes it brittle, because the only path for correction has been engineered out. Override is the moment the human-machine partnership does its most useful work, and the system should be designed to invite it, not punish it.

The practical consequence is that the engineering investment shifts. In traditional automation, the engineering effort goes into the action: making the script do the thing reliably. In agentic systems, an increasing share of the effort goes into the boundary: specifying what the agent may do, encoding the conditions under which it may do it, and instrumenting the demotion paths that tighten autonomy when something is off. The action is increasingly cheap to produce. The boundary is what you are paid to design. Once that inversion is internalised, much of what looks daunting about deploying agents in production becomes a familiar engineering discipline, and one that maps cleanly onto the policies, error budgets, and review processes SRE teams already know how to operate. Trust, in this model, is not a feeling. It is a property of the boundary you built. Like any property of a production system, it is measurable, observable, and improvable.

1.6 From Reactive Operations to Systems That Reason
Most reliability practice today is still reaction-driven. Something breaks, signals fire, humans interpret context, and actions follow. Even in well-run SRE teams this pattern dominates daily work. The difference between mature and immature teams is not whether they react. It is how quickly they can assemble enough context to react well. Agentic reliability engineering does not aim to make reaction faster. It aims to move reasoning earlier in the loop, so that by the time a reaction is needed, most of the work has already been done.

In the reactive model, three fragile assumptions hold the system together. The first is that failures are rare enough for humans to keep up with them. The second is that context can be reconstructed quickly during incidents from logs, dashboards, and recent memory. The third is that experience can compensate for missing signals. At modern scale, none of these survive contact with the production environment. Systems change too frequently for any individual to track. Dependencies are too deep for context to be reconstructed at incident speed. And experience, the most expensive of the three to build, does not transfer cleanly between systems, between teams, or between generations of an architecture.

What replaces reactive operations is not faster reaction. It is continuous reasoning. A reasoning system evaluates current state, recent change, historical patterns, service criticality, declared intent, and the current autonomy boundary, continuously, in the background, at machine speed. From those inputs it forms expectations about what should happen next. When reality diverges from expectation, the response is informed rather than improvised. The system did not wait for an alert to start thinking. It was already thinking, and the alert is the moment its thinking became visible.

This sounds abstract, so it is worth being concrete about what reasoning is and is not in this book. By reasoning we mean policy-constrained decision-making. The agent evaluates structured signals against an explicit model of intent and autonomy boundary, scores its options, and either acts or escalates. We do not mean free-form generation of text, the kind a large language model does when asked to write a paragraph. The two activities are related and the underlying technology often overlaps, but the engineering discipline is different. A reliability decision must be reproducible, auditable, and bounded. These are properties that policy-constrained decision systems are designed to deliver and that pure generation is not.

Note
“Reasoning” in this book means policy-constrained decision-making: scoring options against declared intent, inside an enforced autonomy boundary, with an auditable record of why. It does not mean an LLM “thinking” in free-form prose. The vocabulary collision is real; the engineering discipline is not the same.

It is also worth being honest about what reasoning is not. Correlation is not reasoning. Noticing that two signals move together does not explain causality, intent, or trade-offs. Dashboards are not reasoning. They present, they do not evaluate. Post-incident analysis is not reasoning. It is retrospective, and a system that only learns after failure remains dependent on failure as its learning trigger. Prediction is not reasoning either. Forecasting load or failure probability is useful, but prediction without a decision policy does not produce reliability. A system must still decide what to do when predictions conflict, when confidence is low, or when intent changes. Reasoning connects prediction to action through explicit evaluation of context and constraints. When reasoning is misunderstood as any of these neighbouring activities, autonomy disappoints. When it is designed deliberately, autonomy becomes predictable, auditable, and trustworthy.

Once reasoning becomes continuous, the alerting model shifts. Alerts do not disappear, but they change role. In the reactive model, an alert is the first moment the system asks for attention. It is the trigger for everything else. In the reasoning model, an alert is a downstream artefact, the moment the system surfaces a decision it has already been forming. The most useful alerts in an agentic system are not “something is wrong”; they are “I am about to act, here is what and why” or “I cannot act, here is the boundary I am up against.” That shift in the alert’s job is one of the clearest day-to-day signs that reasoning has moved earlier in the loop, and it changes the on-call experience in a way teams notice within weeks. The page is no longer “figure out what is happening.” The page is “here is what is happening, here is what the system has already done, here is the decision the system can’t make alone.”


![image](https://hackmd.io/_uploads/r1yI8Wa-Mg.png)

Humans remain central in the reasoning model, but, as we have already said, above the loop rather than inside it. They define the intent the system is reasoning against. They draw the autonomy boundary the system is reasoning within. They review the outcomes the system produces and adjust the boundary and the intent in response. They handle the novel situations the system surfaces because those situations fall outside the model. None of this work goes away when reasoning becomes continuous. It becomes more important, because it is now the leverage point that shapes everything the system does autonomously. A team that gets the intent right and the boundary right gets compounding returns on every decision the system makes downstream. A team that gets either of them wrong gets compounding errors at the same rate.

The whole shift, in one sentence. This is what it means to scale judgment, not execution. We are not asking the system to do more of what humans used to do. We are asking it to take on the part of the loop that runs at machine speed (perception, scoring, action) so that humans can take on the part that requires what only humans can give, which is judgment about what the system should be doing in the first place. That sentence is the bumper sticker of the whole book; you will see it once more, at the end of this chapter, and then it does its work in silence.

1.7 Wrap Up: Reframing Reliability for the Agentic Age
This chapter set out to challenge a familiar assumption: that reliability is primarily about reacting well when things go wrong. What emerged instead was a different framing. Reliability at modern scale is no longer a response problem. It is a design problem, and the design has to admit that the human is no longer fast enough to be the centre of the loop. Once that is admitted, much that looked like exhaustion turns out to have been a category error. The on-call rota was never going to keep up, and asking better humans to absorb more signal was never going to be the answer.

The three things to carry forward into the rest of the book are these. First, the gap is structural, not operational. No amount of better tooling, more training, or stronger process will close it on its own, because the gap is between human-paced sense-making and machine-paced system dynamics, and one of those is not going to slow down. Second, ARE is the name we are giving to the discipline that closes the gap, by building reliability systems that perceive, reason, and act within human-authored intent and machine-enforceable autonomy boundaries, while humans operate above the loop. The vocabulary you will need is already on the page: agentic system, above-the-loop human, intent, autonomy boundary, reasoning. We will not redefine these. We will use them. Third, the rest of the book is a tour of what that discipline looks like in practice, in the order an SRE team would actually build it.

That order is deliberate, and it is the contract this book makes with you. Chapter 2 covers observability, because an agent that cannot see clearly cannot reason clearly, and most of the observability stack you have today was designed for a different consumer. Chapter 3 covers resilience: the loop of detect, reason, act, and learn that turns observability into recovery. Chapter 4 lays the architectural backbone (four planes, a maturity model, and the human-in-the-loop control circuit) that the rest of the book depends on. Chapters 5, 6, 7, and 8 take ARE into the operational workflows where SRE teams already work: incident response, delivery, chaos, pre-incident intelligence. They show what changes when reasoning moves earlier in the loop. Chapters 9, 10, 11, and 12 are the deep technical core: decision intelligence, reasoning patterns, governed autonomy, and simulation. Chapters 13, 14, and 15 are the operating model, the multi-agent architecture, and the agent ecosystem you will end up running. The epilogue returns to the human role, because reliability remains, at the limit, a human enterprise that uses machines to do its work.

This is not a book about replacing engineers. It is a book about giving them back the part of the job they were always best at: the judgment, the design, the accountability. We do this by handing the machine-speed parts of the loop to systems built carefully enough to be trusted with them. We are scaling judgment, not execution. The rest of this book is about how.

Most observability stacks are designed for humans to interpret. That is the wrong audience for the next ten years.

Agentic systems do not fail first at decision-making. They fail first at perception. Before a system can reason, predict, or act with any degree of autonomy, it must be able to reliably observe itself and its environment. Not in the way humans observe systems today through dashboards and alerts, but in a way that allows machines to form context, evaluate conditions, and decide with confidence. This is why observability is not simply an enabling capability for ARE. It is the foundational dependency, and getting the order wrong is the single most common reason early agentic initiatives stall.

The chapter before this argued that the human can no longer sit in the centre of the loop. The agent now does. What the agent can see, and what it can understand about what it sees, becomes the single most important property of the entire reliability system. Everything in the rest of this book depends on it. Resilience, incident response, delivery, governance, reasoning patterns. None of them work if the agent’s perception of the system is unreliable, ambiguous, or built for a different consumer. This is why observability comes first in the book and not third or fourth, where most textbooks place it. The agent’s perception is the precondition for everything else. Get this layer wrong, and every layer above it fails for reasons that look like they belong somewhere else.

In traditional SRE and operations models, observability exists primarily to support humans. Metrics, logs, and traces are designed to answer questions during incidents, to support retrospective analysis, or to validate assumptions after the fact. This model works, imperfectly, because humans are good at compensating for gaps. Engineers infer intent. They tolerate ambiguity. They fill missing context with experience and intuition. Machines cannot do this. An agent can only reason about what it can see, and it can only act responsibly if what it sees is accurate, contextual, and decision-relevant.

This distinction becomes visible the moment teams attempt to introduce autonomy on top of existing observability foundations. In many environments, the observability stack looks healthy on the surface. Dashboards are clean. Alerts are tuned. Key services show stable averages. From a human perspective, the system feels well understood. From a machine perspective, it often is not. Latency metrics may be heavily averaged, hiding tail behaviour that matters for decision timing. Error rates may be aggregated across endpoints with very different business impact. Telemetry may lack ownership, criticality, or dependency metadata because humans already know those relationships. To an engineer, these gaps are manageable. To an agent, they create ambiguity at the exact moment precision is required.

This is where early agentic initiatives quietly fail. Not because the agents are poorly designed, but because the sensing layer was never intended to support reasoning systems. The observability foundation was optimised for attention, not understanding. That is the gap this chapter exists to close. By the end of this chapter, the reader should have a working definition of what telemetry built for agents looks like, why it is different from telemetry built for dashboards, and what concrete steps a team can take to evolve from one to the other without rewriting their stack from scratch.

2.1 Observability Beyond Visibility
Visibility is not understanding. Understanding is not decision readiness.

Most teams believe they have good observability because they can see their systems. They have dashboards, alerts, service maps, and incident timelines. They can answer questions when something goes wrong. That belief is reasonable in a human-operated model. It becomes fragile the moment we ask systems to act on their own. The shift from observability as presentation to observability as input is the defining shift of this chapter. The difference matters because visibility is optimised for attention, while decision readiness is optimised for consequence. These goals are not the same, and the trade-offs they impose are often in direct tension.

Consider a common production setup. A service exposes request latency, error rate, and throughput. Dashboards show rolling averages. Alerts fire when thresholds are crossed. During an incident, engineers drill down, correlate logs and traces, and identify the root cause. The system works because humans bridge the gaps. For an agent, the same setup is incomplete. Averaged latency hides tail behaviour that determines user experience. Aggregated error rates blur critical paths with non-critical ones. Alert thresholds encode human tolerance rather than operational risk. None of these are mistakes. They are optimisations for human cognition. Now introduce an agent tasked with taking early action to protect an SLO. The agent sees an increase in latency, but cannot tell whether the impact is isolated to a low-priority endpoint or degrading a critical journey. It cannot assess whether a dependency is the cause or a symptom. It cannot reason about blast radius because ownership and relationships are not encoded in the telemetry. The result is predictable. The agent either acts conservatively and does nothing useful, or acts aggressively and creates instability. Either way, the failure mode traces back to observability that was visible but not actionable.

This shift demands a different unit of measure. We will call it decision-grade telemetry, and we will use the phrase throughout the rest of the book. Decision-grade telemetry is telemetry built for an agent to act on, not for a human to look at. The distinction is not a small one. A dashboard surfaces what is interesting; decision-grade telemetry surfaces what is decidable. A dashboard tolerates ambiguity because a human can resolve it; decision-grade telemetry does not, because no one is going to resolve it before the agent acts. A dashboard is consumed by interpretation; decision-grade telemetry is consumed by code.

Four properties separate decision-grade telemetry from its alternatives. It is queryable, in the sense that an agent can reach it programmatically at decision time without parsing prose or screen-scraping a UI. It is normalised, in the sense that the same concept has the same representation across services, environments, and teams, so reasoning can compose across them. It is semantically annotated, in the sense that what a signal means is encoded alongside the signal itself, not held implicitly in the head of the person who set up the dashboard. And it is decision-relevant, in the sense that every signal in the decision-grade set exists because some specific decision needs it, and signals that no decision uses are recognised as cost rather than coverage. Telemetry that satisfies all four becomes decision-grade. Telemetry that satisfies only the first becomes a faster way to be confused.

![image](https://hackmd.io/_uploads/B1ecLWTWGx.png)

Note
The test of decision-grade telemetry: if an agent has to parse a string to act on a signal, the signal is not decision-grade. Structured fields, typed values, and explicit semantics are not stylistic preferences here. They are the precondition for any reasoning system that follows.

Human-centred observability asks “what do I need to look at right now?” Agentic observability asks “what decision is safe to make right now?” That distinction reshapes everything downstream. A dashboard that surfaces the top five metrics for a service is effective for a human scanning for anomalies. An agent needs to understand which metrics matter for which decisions, under what conditions, and with what confidence. That requires semantics, not presentation. A single latency metric may be perfectly readable on a chart and unreadable to a machine unless it carries context about which requests it represents, which dependencies are involved, which users or journeys are affected, and how quickly the condition is evolving. Without that context, the signal cannot be safely used for autonomous action.

It is worth working through a concrete example of what decision-readiness looks like in a system designed for it from the outset. Consider a large service-oriented platform supporting multiple user journeys with different criticality levels. Observability is not organised around dashboards first. It is organised around the decisions the system may need to make under stress. Latency is not exposed as a single service-level average. Request latency is captured as endpoint-level distributions, with each distribution carrying explicit tags for service name and version, dependency identity, user journey, and business criticality tier. This lets both humans and agents distinguish a spike on a low-priority reporting endpoint from early degradation on a checkout or authentication path, even when the overall service average is stable. Error signals follow the same principle. Rather than a single error rate, failures are segmented by failure class, request intent, and dependency involvement. This lets the system detect asymmetric failure patterns, such as a dependency returning partial errors that only affect a subset of critical requests. Humans can still view aggregated summaries when they need them. The decision-grade signals remain available and structured underneath.

Saturation is the third class of signal worth getting right. The default is to expose CPU and memory percentages, which are easy to visualise and familiar from a decade of dashboards. The problem is that these are coarse and lag the actual operational reality. By the time CPU is at 90% the queue is already growing, by the time the queue is growing latency has already breached, and by the time latency has breached the SLO is already burning. Intent-aware saturation signals (queue depth relative to acceptable latency, request backlog growth rate, thread pool or connection exhaustion trends) are noisier and less visually appealing, but they are far more predictive. They let an agent recognise the moment the system is about to violate an SLO rather than reacting after the fact. None of this is harder to instrument than CPU. It is harder to remember to instrument, because the dashboards that hung in the operations room for ten years did not need it.

The most insidious failure mode in this transition is false confidence. Dashboards look healthy. Alerts are quiet. The system appears stable. Humans trust what they see, and agents amplify that trust at machine speed and consistency. What was once a tolerable blind spot becomes an automated behaviour. Small modelling errors compound into systemic failures. This is what happens when a team treats observability as a reporting layer and then asks a reasoning system to operate on top of it. The reasoning system is not the problem. The reporting layer is. False confidence is particularly common when metrics are heavily aggregated, when relationships between services are implicit rather than explicit, when ownership is assumed rather than encoded, and when signals were designed for retrospection rather than anticipation. Agentic systems do not question these assumptions. They operationalise them at scale.

This reframing forces uncomfortable conversations. Teams discover that much of their observability investment was optimised for reporting, not reasoning. That does not make it wasted. It does mean it must evolve. The remainder of this chapter is about how. We will look at what makes a signal semantically usable by an agent, how to design telemetry for reasoning systems, how to model the relationships agents need in order to reason about blast radius, and where this whole stack tends to fail when it is built in the wrong order. The first move, before any of that, is to accept the reframe. Observability is no longer something you present. It is something you compute against. Once a team accepts that, the design decisions that follow start to make sense. Until then, every other investment in agentic reliability sits on a foundation that cannot carry it.

2.2 Signals, Semantics, and Decision Relevance
Once observability moves beyond visibility, the next question is not what we can see. It is what the system can understand.

This is the point where many observability programmes stall, because collecting signals is easy and making those signals meaningful for decisions is not. For humans, interpretation happens subconsciously. An experienced engineer sees a latency spike and instinctively filters noise, recalls past incidents, and correlates with recent changes. For an agent, none of that intuition exists unless we explicitly encode it. Signals must therefore carry semantics. Without semantics, data is just measurement. With semantics, it becomes evidence. That distinction is not academic. It is the difference between automation that reacts and autonomy that reasons.

The property that closes the gap is what we will call decision relevance. A signal has decision relevance when, taken together with the other signals an agent has access to, it changes which action the agent will choose. A signal that does not change the choice is not decision-relevant, regardless of how accurate or expensive it is to produce. Most telemetry today is not decision-relevant. Most telemetry today is measurement, captured because it is easy to capture, retained because nobody has built the muscle to argue for deleting it. Decision-relevant telemetry is curated, not exhaustive. The discipline is to know which signal will move the agent’s decision and to keep only those.

The contrast is sharpest when you take a familiar metric and look at it from the agent’s side of the wire. Consider a single latency signal collected from a checkout service. Captured as a service-level average over a one-minute window, with no tags, it looks like this:

  checkout.latency.avg = 142ms
That is a fine number for a dashboard. A human looking at it knows what “checkout” is, knows roughly what a healthy number looks like, knows from context that a one-minute average is what is on screen, and can pull up endpoint-level distributions if anything looks off. An agent looking at it has none of that. Did the average move because the slowest 1% of requests doubled, or because the median drifted up? Which endpoints contributed? Which user journeys are degraded? Is the spike on an authenticated path or an anonymous one? Is a known dependency degrading upstream? The signal cannot answer any of these, so the agent cannot reason about any of these. The signal is not decision-relevant. It is a measurement.

Now the same underlying data, captured semantically:

  service: "checkout"
  endpoint: "/api/v2/cart/submit"
  journey: "purchase"
  criticality: "tier-1"
  latency_p50_ms: 38
  latency_p99_ms: 412
  latency_p999_ms: 1180
  request_count: 8204
  window_start: "2025-11-14T03:14:00Z"
  window_seconds: 60
  upstream_dependencies: ["payments", "inventory"]
  upstream_dependency_health: { "payments": "degraded", "inventory": "ok" }
  recent_change_id: "deploy-3942"
  recent_change_age_seconds: 184
EXAMPLE
The same underlying latency data, captured semantically. p50/p99/p999 instead of a single average. Endpoint, journey, and criticality tags. Upstream-dependency state. The agent can now ask: is p99 degrading on a tier-1 endpoint? Is the upstream dependency in a known degraded state? Did a recent change correlate with the shift? Each of those is a question an agent can act on. The first version supported none of them.

The data volume here is not meaningfully larger than the original. The discipline applied at emission time is what changed. Once a signal carries semantics, the agent stops guessing. It can scope its action to the right endpoint. It can defer when the upstream dependency is the more likely cause. It can correlate the latency shift with a deploy that landed three minutes ago. Each of those is a decision, not a measurement, and decisions are what agentic systems are built to produce. This is the whole job of decision-grade telemetry, made concrete on a single signal.

Three further properties of semantics are worth naming explicitly here, because they are what most teams under-invest in. The first is that semantics must be consistent across emitters. A latency signal that means one thing on the checkout service and a different thing on the search service will produce contradictory reasoning. The schema is the contract, and the contract must be enforceable in code at emission time, not in a wiki at debugging time. The second is that semantics must be versioned. Schemas evolve. Agents trained or configured against one version of the schema will misread signals emitted against another. Telemetry needs the same release discipline as production code. The teams that treat it as configuration tend to discover this the hard way. The third is that decision-relevance is conditional, not absolute. Not every signal matters for every decision. An increase in error rate during a deployment may indicate a failed release. The same increase during a controlled chaos experiment may be expected. An agent that cannot distinguish between these contexts will either overreact or underreact. Decision-relevance therefore depends on conditions, and conditions must be encoded in the signal stream just as carefully as the measurements themselves. Deployment state, experiment state, ownership boundaries, and policy constraints all belong in the telemetry, because all of them change what a given signal means.

Confidence and uncertainty deserve their own beat here, because they are the place where agentic observability differs most starkly from the traditional kind. Human operators are comfortable with uncertainty. We routinely say things like “this looks suspicious” or “I am not convinced this is the root cause yet.” Traditional monitoring tools do not model this nuance. They present binary states. Healthy or unhealthy. Alerting or not. Agentic systems cannot rely on binary interpretations alone. They must reason about confidence. A signal may suggest a problem with low certainty. Another may strongly indicate impact but only for a subset of users. Decision logic must account for this gradient. This is where observability data intersects with probability and inference. Signals should be designed to support confidence estimation. That means historical baselines, variance measures, correlation strength, or explicit uncertainty bounds emitted alongside the point values. When confidence is not modelled, agents tend toward one of two failure modes. They act too aggressively, triggering unnecessary interventions, or they become overly conservative and require human approval for everything. Both outcomes undermine autonomy. Neither is fixable with a better model. The fix is in the signal.

There is one final risk worth flagging before we leave this section. When semantics are missing or inconsistent, the failure mode is not visible immediately. The agent acts on the signal it has, and the signal is plausible, and the action looks reasonable, and the system continues running. The system has, in fact, become confidently wrong, and the cost of that wrongness accumulates silently until something breaks in a way that is no longer explainable. The next chapter on resilience treats this as a first-class concern. For now the warning is that decision-relevance is not optional. A reasoning system without semantically grounded inputs is a fast guessing machine. Sometimes it guesses right. Sometimes it does not. Either way, it is not what the book is about.

2.3 Designing Telemetry for Reasoning Systems
Knowing that telemetry must be decision-grade is one thing. Designing it that way from the start is another.

Most teams begin with an existing observability stack and try to retrofit decision-relevance into it. That is fine, and it is the path most teams will actually take. But the design rules are clearest when you state them as if you were starting from a blank slate. Four rules cover most of what matters, and each maps to one of the four properties of decision-grade telemetry from section 2.1.

The first rule is machine-parseable by default. Every signal is emitted as a structured record with typed fields, not as a string that needs to be parsed. Logs follow a schema. Metrics carry their labels as structured key-value pairs. Traces have their semantic tags attached at the span, not buried in a message field. An agent should never have to apply a regex to act on a signal. If it has to, the signal is not decision-grade and the cost of using it scales with every new agent you connect. The discipline here is mechanical, not architectural. It costs an extra hour at instrumentation time and saves weeks at integration time. In practice this often means adopting a unified telemetry model such as OpenTelemetry, not because it is fashionable but because it forces consistency in how signals, traces, and metadata are expressed. Metrics, traces, and logs become different views over the same underlying events rather than separate streams stitched together at read time. That consistency is what allows agents to compose signals across telemetry types without each agent reimplementing the join.

The second rule is contextual by default. Every signal carries the context required to interpret it in the same record. A latency reading carries its endpoint, journey, criticality, and dependency context. An error record carries its failure class, request intent, and dependency identity. A saturation signal carries the queue or pool whose saturation it represents, and the SLO that queue depth is judged against. The shape of context is domain-specific. The principle is universal. If interpreting the signal requires joining it against a separate system at read time, that join becomes a latency tax on every decision the agent makes, and worse, becomes a source of failure when the separate system is itself degraded. Push the context into the signal, not into the consumer. Histograms instead of averages. Labels that encode ownership and criticality at emission time. Aggregation applied deliberately rather than by default. None of this requires new tooling. It requires the team to model its data as if a machine were going to act on it, because one increasingly is.

The third rule is time-coherent. Signals from different sources must align on time in a way that supports causal reasoning. Clocks are NTP-synced. Timestamps are emitted at the source, not assigned at ingest. Windows are explicit. The signal emitter declares its own observation window rather than relying on the consumer to infer it. This sounds boring, and it is, until you watch an agent attribute a latency spike to the wrong deploy because the deploy event arrived at the observability pipeline forty seconds after the latency signal it caused. Time-coherence is what turns a stream of measurements into a story the agent can read. Distributed tracing systems become particularly powerful in this model, because traces enriched with dependency and intent metadata give the agent the causal context that loose metrics alone cannot. Rather than asking “is the service slow,” the agent reasons about “which dependency is degrading, for which journey, with what impact.” That is a different question, and it is only askable when time is honest.

The fourth rule is lineage-preserving. Every signal can be traced back to its origin. Which service emitted it. Which version of which schema produced it. Which infrastructure pipeline carried it. When an agent’s reasoning depends on a signal, the agent should be able to inspect the signal’s provenance. This matters more than it sounds, because the worst class of agentic failure is the one where the agent acted correctly on data that turned out to be wrong. Lineage is what makes that failure debuggable. Without it, the agent’s reasoning is a black box and so is the data it reasoned over. Lineage also matters at audit time. When a regulator, an executive, or a peer team asks why an agent took a particular action, the team’s answer cannot be “the signal looked like this and we trust the pipeline.” The answer must be reconstructable from records, with the schema version, the source, and the path the data took all retrievable. Treat lineage as a feature of the telemetry, not as something the observability vendor might support in a future release.

Together, these four rules produce telemetry that is queryable, normalised, semantically annotated, and decision-relevant. They also produce a property the chapter has not named yet but needs to. The reliability of telemetry itself becomes something we measure. Coverage, freshness, semantic completeness, schema conformance. These are not nice-to-haves. They are the first-class signals an agent needs in order to know whether its other signals can be trusted. We will call telemetry about telemetry-quality an agentic SLI, and the book will return to the concept repeatedly. A team that has not measured the quality of its observability stack is asking its agent to act on signals whose reliability it cannot characterise. That is not a stance an SRE team would accept on a service. There is no reason to accept it on the telemetry that drives the agents now operating that service.

Tip
The one-line agentic-SLI test: can I query the freshness, completeness, and provenance of this signal in the same way I query the signal itself? If yes, the signal is operable. If no, the signal is decoration.

The shift these rules describe is, at root, a shift in audience. Telemetry built for dashboards is built to be read. Telemetry built for reasoning systems is built to be computed against. Most observability investments to date have been the first. The next decade of observability work is the second. Teams that get this right will find that their existing stacks support most of what they need, applied with different discipline. Teams that do not will find themselves rebuilding the same stack three times, each time blaming the tool. The platform is rarely the constraint. The schema is. The conventions are. The willingness to treat the observability layer as a production system is. A team that gets those three right does not need to migrate its telemetry stack. A team that gets them wrong will migrate three times and still not have decision-grade telemetry on the other side.

2.4 Relationships, Dependency Graphs, and Ownership Intelligence
Decision-grade signals are necessary. They are not sufficient. A reasoning system does not act on signals in isolation. It acts on signals in relation to one another, and the relationships are what carry most of the meaning.

In human operations, dependency knowledge lives in people’s heads. Senior engineers know which services are fragile, which dependencies are critical, and which failures cascade. That knowledge rarely exists in a form machines can consume. As long as humans are the primary decision-makers, this gap is survivable. Once agents enter the loop, the gap becomes a hard boundary. An agent cannot reason about blast radius from telemetry alone. It needs a structure that names what depends on what, in which direction, with what role, and under whose ownership. That structure is the topology graph.

The topology graph is the model of the system that agents query when they reason. Services are nodes. Dependencies are typed edges. Each edge carries direction (which component depends on which), role (synchronous call, asynchronous event, cache lookup, optional enrichment), and criticality (does failure of this edge block a user journey, degrade it, or simply log a warning). Ownership sits as metadata on the nodes, encoding who is accountable and which escalation path applies. Change events flow over the graph as a stream of facts that the agent can correlate against current state. The graph is to agentic reasoning what the service map is to human troubleshooting, but with the precision a machine needs in order to act rather than just look.

The chapter will return to one of these graphs throughout the rest of the book under the name of the Topology Agent. The Topology Agent owns the graph. It keeps the graph current. It exposes a query interface that other agents can call when they need to reason about a service’s neighbourhood. When an Incident Agent needs to know whether a latency spike on checkout might be the result of an upstream payments degradation, it queries the Topology Agent. When a Change Agent needs to know which services share a fate with the one it is about to deploy, it queries the Topology Agent. When a Resource Allocation Agent needs to know whether scaling a service will affect a downstream dependency, it queries the Topology Agent. The Topology Agent is the system’s memory of its own structure, and every other agent in this book depends on it.

Note
The Topology Agent is the first named member of the agent cast that runs throughout this book. It will return in Chapters 5, 8, 14, and 15. For now, treat it as the named owner of the topology graph. When an agent reasons about relationships, it is reasoning against the Topology Agent’s model.

A useful way to make this concrete is to look at the kind of query an agent runs against the graph during an incident. Suppose a latency alert fires on a service called `inventory-api`. The Incident Agent does not need to look at `inventory-api` in isolation. It needs to know what `inventory-api` is connected to and what those connections look like. A query against the Topology Agent might return something like this:

  service: "inventory-api"
  owners: ["team-platform"]
  tier: "tier-1"
  upstream_dependencies:
	- service: "warehouse-db",  	role: "blocking", criticality: "critical"
	- service: "cache-cluster", 	role: "fallback", criticality: "degraded-ok"
	- service: "feature-flags", 	role: "optional", criticality: "tolerable"
  downstream_consumers:
	- service: "checkout-api",  	journey: "purchase", 	impact: "blocks"
	- service: "search-api",    	journey: "browse",   	impact: "degrades"
	- service: "reporting-job", 	journey: "internal", 	impact: "delays"
  recent_changes:
	- change_id: "deploy-3942", 	age_seconds: 184, scope: "inventory-api"
	- change_id: "config-1027", 	age_seconds: 612, scope: "warehouse-db"
What this lets the agent reason about is the difference between a degradation that blocks a tier-1 user journey and one that delays an internal reporting job. The first is worth acting on aggressively. The second is worth deferring. The agent does not have to be told this. It can reason its way to the right action from the graph and the criticality tags. The signal alone could not support that reasoning. The graph is what makes the reasoning possible.

![image](https://hackmd.io/_uploads/Sk6iUW6ZMx.png)


The graph itself can be built two ways, and most mature teams do both. Some of it is inferred from telemetry. Distributed traces reveal call paths. Events reveal coordination. Configuration state reveals intended topology. Inference scales well, but it produces noisy edges when traffic is bursty, retries are aggressive, or fallbacks fire under stress. The other path is to declare dependencies explicitly. Teams who own a service declare what it depends on, with role and criticality tags, as part of the service’s configuration. Declared dependencies are validated against observed traces, so the graph stays honest. The combination produces a graph that is both complete and accurate. Either approach alone tends to drift.

The trade-off between these two paths is worth understanding before a team commits to one. Inferred graphs are cheap to start and require no coordination with service-owning teams. Their failure mode is that they capture transient edges (retries, fallbacks, rare-path lookups) with the same weight as canonical ones, and a graph that promotes every observed call to a “real” dependency will produce reasoning that treats noise as structure. The fix is edge weighting and stability filters, which work, but only after the graph has been live long enough to learn what stable looks like. Declared graphs are the opposite. They demand upfront discipline from every team that owns a service, but the resulting graph is authoritative from day one. The failure mode is that declared dependencies drift from reality. A team adds a new dependency in code and forgets to update the declaration. The graph believes the world is one shape while the system runs in another. Validation against observed traces closes this loop, but only if the validation is treated as a production signal rather than as a nice-to-have. Most teams that get to a working graph use inference to bootstrap and declaration to anchor, with continuous reconciliation between the two.

Ownership intelligence is the layer that sits on top of the graph and makes its actions safe. Knowing what a service depends on tells you what might break. Knowing who owns it tells you who needs to be involved when something does. Ownership matters for two reasons. It encodes the social contract about which team is accountable, which an agent must respect when deciding whether to act unilaterally or escalate. And it encodes the boundary of which actions the agent is permitted to take without coordination. An agent that isolates a third-party dependency owned by a different team without consultation is not optimising. It is causing a governance incident. The Topology Agent’s ownership metadata is what prevents this. The agent reads ownership, the action policy checks ownership against the agent’s authority, and the action either fires or routes for review.

The combination of a typed dependency graph and ownership metadata is what turns isolated signals into a system the agent can reason about. Without it, the agent is acting on disconnected measurements and hoping the connections it infers are real. With it, the agent has the same structural awareness that a senior on-call engineer brings to an incident, encoded in a form a machine can query in milliseconds. This is the structural prerequisite for everything in Part II of this book, and it is the single most under-invested layer in most observability stacks today. A team that treats topology as a wiki page is asking its agents to operate blind. A team that treats topology as a queryable, owned, version-controlled artefact is doing the foundational work that everything else builds on.

2.5 Control, Governance, and Decision Boundaries
Observability and governance touch each other at every point, and they are not the same thing. Decision-grade telemetry tells the agent what is happening. The autonomy boundary, introduced in section 1.5, tells the agent what it is permitted to do about it. The Governance Plane, which we will detail in section 4.6, is the engineered surface where those permissions live. Each of these layers is a separate chapter’s worth of work, and we will not collapse them into this section. What matters here is the handoff. The observability stack produces the signals that the governance layer evaluates against policy at decision time. If the signals are not decision-grade, the governance evaluations are not trustworthy. If the governance layer is not present, decision-grade signals are wasted. The two evolve together, and they evolve at the same maturity level. A team that has invested heavily in one but not the other will find that the under-invested layer becomes the binding constraint on what their agents can safely do, no matter how mature the other layer becomes. We will pick this up properly when we reach the architecture chapter.

2.6 Failure Modes, Limits, and Anti-Patterns
Up to this point the chapter has been about what agentic observability looks like when it works. This section does the opposite. Five failure modes show up consistently across teams attempting this work, and each one undermines agentic reliability in a different way. Naming them is the first step to recognising them in your own stack.

These failure modes share a property worth stating before we name them individually. Each of them is a design failure rather than a tooling failure. Better dashboards do not fix any of them. Better alert routing does not fix any of them. The failures live in how the team decided to model its data, where it decided to put context, and which signals it decided were worth investing in. That makes them stubborn. It also makes them recognisable, and it means a team that can name them in its own stack can fix them with the discipline it already has, without buying anything new.

The first is silent decay. The signals were decision-grade when the team built them. Schemas drift over time. New services emit with old conventions. A field that used to be required becomes optional. A criticality tag stops being maintained. None of this fires an alarm because nothing is broken from a measurement standpoint. Metrics still flow. Dashboards still render. The agent’s reasoning quality, however, degrades on a curve that nobody is watching. By the time someone notices, the agent has been making subtly worse decisions for weeks. Silent decay is the failure mode that justifies the agentic-SLI discipline from section 2.3. You cannot manage what you do not measure, and most teams do not measure the reliability of their own telemetry.

The second is stale topology. The graph was accurate when it was built. New services come online. Dependencies are added. Ownership changes hands. Some of these update the graph automatically through CI. Others rely on someone remembering to update a declaration. The graph drifts, and the agent reasons against the stale version, and the actions it chooses are based on a system that no longer exists. The fix here is not heroics. It is treating the topology graph as a production artefact with the same release discipline as code, and validating declared dependencies against observed telemetry continuously rather than at deploy time only.

The third is ambiguous semantics. A latency signal on one service includes time spent in upstream calls. The same signal on another service excludes it. A user-facing error metric counts retries as failures on one team’s service and as successful eventual completions on another’s. The metrics carry the same field names. The semantics underneath them differ. An agent reasoning across services will produce contradictory conclusions and have no way to detect that the contradiction is in the data, not the world. This is what makes consistent schemas and emission contracts so important. Convention without enforcement is decoration.

The fourth is signal flood. The team has instrumented aggressively. Thousands of metrics per service. Logs that capture every code path. Traces that record every hop. The signal-to-noise ratio collapses. Humans deal with this by ignoring most of it, which works for humans and does not work for agents. An agent presented with a flood of signals either becomes overwhelmingly conservative (escalate everything because confidence never stabilises) or oscillates between actions as different signals contradict one another. The discipline here is to curate, not collect. Every signal in the decision-grade set should exist because some specific decision needs it. Signals that no decision uses should be moved out of the decision set, kept for forensics if needed, and not be presented to the agent at all.

The fifth is signal cliff. The opposite of signal flood, and equally dangerous. The team has trimmed aggressively and stripped out signals that the agent now needs in order to reason about novel conditions. Under normal load, everything looks fine. Under a previously unseen failure mode, the agent has no signals that distinguish this failure from anything else, and either acts wrongly or escalates everything. The corrective is to design the decision-grade set against the space of decisions the agent might need to make, not just the decisions it makes today. A small reserve of signals that exist for situations the agent has not yet encountered is not waste. It is the same investment a chess engine makes when it evaluates positions it does not expect to see.

![image](https://hackmd.io/_uploads/HJATIbpWGe.png)

Warning
The worst observability failure for an agentic system is not absence of signal. It is confidently wrong signal. Absence of signal makes the agent uncertain, and an uncertain agent escalates. Confidently wrong signal makes the agent decisive about the wrong action. The first is recoverable. The second can take a system down before anyone realises the data was the problem.

These five failure modes share a second structural property worth naming explicitly. Each of them is invisible from the dashboard. Silent decay does not fire an alert. Stale topology does not show up in latency. Ambiguous semantics does not flag itself. Signal flood and signal cliff both look like normal operations until the moment they don’t. This is why agentic observability has to measure itself. The agent’s reliability is downstream of the observability layer’s reliability, and the observability layer’s reliability is downstream of someone deciding it is worth measuring. Section 2.8 turns this into a set of concrete agentic SLIs that any team can adopt. The starting point is recognising that the five failures above are real, common, and not detectable by the tools that worked when the only consumer was a human.

2.7 Maturity Prerequisites and Readiness Signals
ARE is earned in levels, as section 1.3 argued. The observability profile a team needs at each level is different, and trying to skip the lower levels produces the failure modes section 2.6 just catalogued. Chapter 4.9 will lay out the full maturity model. The observability projection of that model is short enough to fit here.

At L1, reactive, observability exists primarily as dashboards and alerts. Telemetry is human-consumed. Schemas are inconsistent across services. Ownership lives in wikis. This is the starting point for most organisations and there is nothing wrong with it as a starting point. Agents introduced at this level will not produce returns; they will produce surprises.

At L2, instrumented, telemetry exists at sufficient coverage to answer most operational questions, and a meaningful share of it is structured. Schemas exist, at least for the highest-traffic services. Some ownership metadata is encoded in catalogues rather than only documented. Topology exists in some form, even if it is partly inferred. This is the level at which agentic experiments become reasonable, scoped narrowly to specific decisions.

At L3, intent-aware, the agentic-SLI discipline has been adopted. Telemetry quality is itself measured. Schemas are versioned and enforced. The topology graph is queryable and validated. Intent is expressed alongside thresholds. The team has decision-grade telemetry for the workflows that matter most, even if not for every service. This is the level at which agents start to compound.

At L4, bounded autonomy, decision-grade telemetry is the default. New services emit to the schema as part of their bootstrap. The topology graph is treated as a production artefact. Ownership is queryable in the same shape as telemetry. Autonomy boundaries are encoded as policy and evaluated against the same telemetry that drives reasoning. The agent’s actions are auditable end-to-end from signal through decision to outcome.

At L5, compounding, the system is reasoning about its own observability. Coverage gaps are detected automatically. Schemas evolve through review. The Learning Agent (Chapter 15.10) closes the loop between outcomes and signal design, so that signals which never inform a decision are flagged for removal and decisions made under low-confidence signals trigger investment in better ones. This is the steady state, and very few organisations operate here yet. The maturity model exists in part to describe what it would look like if they did.

The point of stating these compactly is not to grade teams. It is to let a team locate itself honestly and to know what to invest in next. Every level transition is concrete observability work, mostly unglamorous, mostly schema and pipeline and graph rather than model and prompt. A team that wants the L4 returns from an L2 observability stack will not get them. A team that wants to know which level it is at can use the agentic SLIs in the next section to find out.

2.8 Defining and Measuring What “Good” Looks Like
If telemetry is the substrate that agentic reliability runs on, then the quality of that substrate is a first-class property of the production system. Treat it accordingly. Five concrete properties, each measurable, separate decision-grade observability in practice from telemetry that merely claims the label. These properties are operational. A team that wants to know whether it is ready for the next level of autonomy can measure them today and answer honestly.

The first property is signal completeness. The fraction of decisions an agent might need to make for which the required signals are present and decision-relevant. A practical measurement: for each named decision in the agent’s responsibility set, declare the signals required to make that decision. Measure the percentage of decisions whose required signals are all present, fresh, and conform to the expected schema. A target of 98% is appropriate for tier-1 workflows; lower targets are reasonable for newer or less critical ones. What is not appropriate is not measuring completeness at all, and discovering at decision time that a required signal was never wired up.

The second property is semantic coherence. The fraction of signals that conform to the declared schema and consistent semantic conventions. A practical measurement: emit schema-conformance metrics from the telemetry pipeline itself. Every signal that fails validation is counted. The target is 99.9% or better, and the residual 0.1% is captured for investigation rather than dropped silently. Teams that find their conformance rate below 99% will also find that their agents make confident decisions on signals that mean different things on different services, which is the ambiguous-semantics failure mode from 2.6.

The third property is temporal fidelity. The fraction of signal pairs whose timestamps agree to within a tolerance appropriate for the decisions that depend on them. A practical measurement: pick a high-volume signal pair that the agent reasons across (e.g., a deploy event and the latency signals from the affected service). Measure the offset between source timestamps and the times those signals arrived at the reasoning layer. Targets here are domain-specific; what matters is that the team has measured and accepted the offset, rather than discovering it during a confidently wrong root-cause analysis.

The fourth property is lineage availability. The fraction of decisions whose reasoning trace can be reconstructed end-to-end, from the original signals through the inference and policy evaluation to the action that fired. A practical measurement: for every action the agent takes, capture the inputs, the policy evaluations, the confidence scores, and the outcome. Then take a sample of actions and verify that the lineage actually reconstructs. A target of 100% is appropriate here, because reasoning that cannot be reconstructed cannot be debugged, and reasoning that cannot be debugged should not be running in production.

The fifth property is outcome attribution. The fraction of agentic actions for which the actual outcome (did the action reduce impact, have no effect, or worsen the situation) can be measured against the predicted outcome at decision time. A practical measurement: every action declares an expected outcome with a confidence score; the system measures the realised outcome against that prediction. The aggregate across actions becomes the agent’s calibration score. A well-calibrated agent has a measurable confidence-to-correctness mapping. A poorly calibrated agent has actions that fire confidently and fail silently. Calibration is what the resilience chapter (3) and the simulation chapter (12) build on. Without outcome attribution, neither chapter can run its closed loop.

EXAMPLE
A worked agentic SLI for tier-1 incident response: 98% of incidents have full topology context and recent change context attached within 30 seconds of detection. Three things to notice. The SLI is operational, not aspirational. It declares a window (30 seconds), a target (98%), and the precise inputs the agent needs (topology context, recent change context). When the SLI burns, the team knows the agent is operating with degraded inputs, and the autonomy boundary should tighten until the SLI recovers.

These five properties become the agentic SLIs the team operates against. None of them require exotic tooling. All of them require schema discipline, lineage capture at emission time, and a willingness to treat the observability layer as a production system whose reliability matters in its own right. A team that adopts these five SLIs and operates against them will discover, often within a quarter, that the foundation it thought it had needs work the team had not previously considered foundational. That discovery is the point. The alternative is to discover the same gaps under the pressure of an outage, with the agent confidently making decisions on signals nobody verified.

What changes operationally when these SLIs are in place is worth naming. Alert volume tends to decrease as the team curates the decision-grade signal set, because alerts move from “anomaly detected” to “decision required and the agent cannot make it alone.” Engineers come to trust automated assessments even when they choose not to act on them, because the assessments arrive with lineage, with confidence scores, and with the topology and change context that an agent’s reasoning relied on. Incidents start to feel explainable rather than chaotic. Governance shifts in shape too. The conversation moves from “should this action be allowed” to “what intent should this agent be operating under.” The system does not feel clever in any of this. It feels calm and predictable, which is what good observability has always felt like when it was working. The only thing that has changed is who is consuming it.

There is one more dimension worth flagging here, because it is what makes these SLIs durable rather than a one-time audit. The reliability of the observability layer changes over time, both as the system grows and as agents are added or modified. The SLIs are not a target to be hit once and then claimed. They are a continuous property of the system, measured the same way uptime is measured, owned the same way uptime is owned, and reviewed the same way uptime is reviewed. A team that builds the SLI scaffolding for a launch and then lets it lapse will rediscover the problems the SLIs were supposed to surface, under conditions that make them harder to address. The discipline is the deliverable. The dashboard is just the surface.

2.9 From Foundations to Action
Observability for humans and observability for machines are not the same thing. That is the single sentence this chapter exists to land. Everything from decision-grade telemetry through agentic SLIs, the topology graph, the Topology Agent, the five failure modes, and the maturity prerequisites follows from that one shift. A team that internalises it will design differently from one that does not, and the difference compounds with every workflow that follows.

The throughline is simple. Autonomy is only as good as the clarity of the system it operates within. Signal volume does not equal signal quality. Ownership, intent, and governance must be observable in machine-readable form, not documented in a wiki. Maturity compounds, and discipline at the foundation determines whether each level above it produces returns or surprises. Good observability for agentic systems feels calm. It reduces surprise, not just reaction time. Teams that internalise this stop chasing tooling maturity and start designing systems that can reason alongside them.

What sits downstream of this chapter is the loop that turns observation into reliability. Detect, reason, act, learn. The first beat of that loop runs against the observability layer this chapter has been about. The signals decide what the agent perceives. The topology decides what the agent reasons over. The autonomy boundary, which is governance work, decides what the agent is permitted to do. The outcome attribution decides what the agent learns. Each of those layers fails differently when observability is unprepared, and each of them compounds differently when observability is right. The next chapter installs that loop formally, names its parts, and shows how the parts fit together to produce a system that improves through experience rather than through heroics. Nothing in that chapter works without what you have just built here. This chapter was about earning the right to learn from failure. The next is about turning that learning into self-healing.
