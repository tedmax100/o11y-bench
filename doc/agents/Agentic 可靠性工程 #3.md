# Chapter 6. The Agentic Reliability Architecture
A Note for Early Release Readers

This will be the 4th chapter of the final book. Please note that the GitHub repo will be made active later on.

Most AIOps initiatives fail because they add intelligence to existing tools rather than designing the system intelligence can safely operate within. That sentence summarises a decade of pattern observation. The teams that wire smart components onto existing toolchains find themselves explaining, eighteen months in, why the agent’s behaviour is inconsistent, why trust is eroding rather than growing, and why the same class of incident keeps producing different responses. The teams that design the system first do not have those conversations. They have different conversations, harder ones, but the harder conversations are the ones that lead somewhere.

Autonomy is not a feature you enable. It is a property that emerges when a system is structured to observe itself clearly, reason within defined constraints, and act without exceeding delegated authority. Without that structure, intelligent components amplify whatever shape already exists. If the system is fragmented, autonomy fragments with it. If ownership is unclear, agents make confident but misaligned decisions. If governance lives outside the runtime, learning becomes dangerous rather than useful. This is why architecture comes before workflows in this book, even though the workflows are where autonomy is most visibly useful. The instinct to jump straight to incident response (Chapter 5) is the right one in spirit. The wrong order is the architectural mistake. This chapter exists to make the structure explicit before any workflow is layered on top of it.

The reader’s first reaction may be to push back on this ordering. Incident response is painful. Change management is slow. Chaos engineering feels manual. These are natural places to apply agentic behaviour. But applying autonomy to workflows before defining architecture is like building on shifting ground. Each workflow invents its own patterns. Constraints diverge. Learning becomes fragmented. Over time, the system becomes harder to reason about rather than easier. Every chapter in Part II will reference back to the architecture this chapter installs. The architectural primitives appear in incident response, delivery, chaos, and pre-incident workflows alike. Once you see them here, you will recognise them everywhere else, and the recognition is what allows the workflow chapters to be useful as references rather than as standalone tutorials.

What this chapter delivers, concretely, is a reference shape. Four planes that separate signal, reasoning, execution, and governance. A canonical treatment of human-in-the-loop as a designed control circuit rather than a fallback. A worked reference architecture that shows the planes interacting through one scenario. And a maturity model that tells the reader what must be true at each level before the next level is safe. None of this is a vendor template. It is the minimum architectural shape required for agentic reliability to function safely, expressed at the level a practitioner can adapt to their own environment without re-inventing the structural decisions.

Note
A well-designed agentic reliability architecture provides three guarantees. Decisions are explainable: signals are structured, context is preserved, reasoning paths are visible. Actions are bounded: every execution path is constrained by explicit policy and confidence thresholds. Learning is safe: feedback loops improve future behaviour without hiding problems or eroding accountability. An architecture that does not provide all three will produce autonomy that feels reckless rather than engineered, and the difference between the two is what determines whether the system will be trusted at scale.

In traditional reliability engineering, trust is social. Engineers trust one another based on experience, shared understanding, and established norms. That trust is reinforced through reviews, incident processes, and culture. In agentic systems, trust must also be technical. The system must be able to prove why it acted, what constraints it respected, and how it will behave next time. This proof cannot rely on post-incident analysis alone. It must be observable at runtime. Architecture is what makes that possible, and it is what the rest of this chapter develops.

The other observation worth making upfront is that this chapter is descriptive of structure, not prescriptive of vendors. There is no single correct platform stack for an agentic reliability architecture. There are several valid implementation patterns for each plane, and we will name them where they differ in important ways, but the architectural shape is what matters, not the tool choice. A team using OpenTelemetry, Prometheus, OPA, and a homegrown reasoning service can produce a clean four-plane architecture. So can a team using a different stack entirely. What matters is that the structural separations described in this chapter are present and enforceable in whatever stack the team chooses. The structure is the invariant; the implementation is local.

4.1 From Tools to Systems: Reframing the Reliability Stack
Most reliability stacks today are assembled, not designed. Over time, teams add monitoring tools to gain visibility, alerting tools to detect failure, ticketing systems to coordinate response, automation to reduce toil, and governance processes to manage risk. Each component solves a real problem. Together, they form what looks like a reliability system. In practice, they behave more like a toolchain, and the difference between a toolchain and a system is what this section is about.

A toolchain depends on humans to connect context, interpret signals, decide what matters, and orchestrate action across boundaries. The stack produces data, but meaning is constructed externally. Automation executes instructions, but does not understand intent. Governance exists, but largely outside the runtime. This model has worked for the last two decades because humans have acted as the integration layer. We will use that phrase deliberately. The integration layer is where context lives, where decisions get made, where boundaries between tools become meaningful. In every reliability stack you have ever worked with, the integration layer has been the humans on call. They have done it well enough that the cost of that integration was never properly visible. The cost is visible now, because the integration layer no longer scales.

To make this concrete, picture a tier-1 incident in a mid-sized engineering organisation today. An alert fires in the monitoring tool, paging the on-call engineer. They open the dashboard, scan recent metrics, and notice latency on the checkout service. They jump to the tracing tool to find which dependency is slow, then to the deployment system to check whether anything shipped in the last hour, then to the chat channel to ask whether anyone else has noticed, then to the topology diagram (probably a wiki page, probably out of date) to figure out blast radius, then back to the monitoring tool to validate the hypothesis, then to the runbook (also a wiki page) to find the recommended mitigation, then to the cloud console to execute it, then back to monitoring to watch for recovery. Six tools, ten minutes, one human holding all the context in their head. The tools each did their jobs. The human did the work that turned them into a system. When the same incident has to be handled by an agent, none of that head-context exists. The agent inherits the fragmentation, not the integration. This is what Agentic Reliability Engineering forces every team to confront.

The failure modes here are predictable. Without a coherent runtime environment where system state is machine-readable, intent is explicit, ownership is discoverable, action authority is enforceable, and outcomes are measured, autonomy fails in one of three ways. It becomes advisory-only, producing suggestions humans ignore under pressure. It becomes overconfident, taking actions without sufficient grounding or constraint. Or it becomes inconsistent, behaving differently in similar conditions because the context it depends on is incomplete or drifting. None of these is a model problem. All of them are system-design problems, and the system in question is the reliability stack itself.

The reframe is straightforward to state and harder to apply. Reliability is not a set of tools. It is a system that must exhibit behaviour. Instead of asking what tools you use, the better questions are: how does the system represent its state in a way machines can reason over, how does it encode intent and trade-offs (performance, cost, risk), how does it enforce boundaries at runtime, how does it prove why it acted, and how does it learn safely without hiding ownership. These are architecture-level questions, not tool-level questions, and most reliability stacks today cannot answer them coherently. That is the gap the four planes of section 4.2 are designed to close.

This reframe has a second consequence that deserves naming explicitly, because it is the one most teams underestimate. When the integration layer was human, the cost of that integration was paid in the time, attention, and judgement of the engineers doing it. The cost was real but distributed and invisible to budgeting processes. When the integration layer becomes the system itself, the cost moves into the system’s design: schemas, contracts, policies, observability of the system’s own behaviour. The total cost does not go down. It moves from an operational expense (engineer time during incidents) to a structural expense (engineering work to design the integration). Teams that expect agentic adoption to be free because “the agent does the work the human used to do” are usually disappointed. The right framing is that agentic adoption relocates the cost from operational hours into architectural investment, and the architectural investment compounds in a way operational hours never do.

Note
When an agent operates across a fragmented toolchain, it inherits the fragmentation, not the integration. This is the single most under-appreciated property of early agentic adoption. Teams expect to gain integration by adding the agent. They gain consistency only when they design the integration into the system first.

The architectural principle that follows from this reframe is that the same primitives must apply across workflows. The signals that drive incident response are the same signals that drive change management. The action contracts that govern recovery are the same action contracts that govern delivery rollback. The governance policies that bound autonomy during an incident are the same policies that bound it during a chaos experiment. If each workflow invents its own version of these primitives, the system fragments along workflow lines and learning cannot compound. If the primitives are shared, every workflow benefits from improvements made in any other workflow. This is the structural reason architecture comes first. Every chapter in Part II runs on the architecture this chapter installs.

One last observation about migration paths. Most teams cannot rebuild their reliability stack from scratch, and the architecture this chapter proposes does not require them to. The four planes can be installed incrementally onto an existing stack. The Signal Plane often starts as a structured-emission discipline applied to existing telemetry, with signal contracts wrapping the streams a team already produces. The Reasoning Plane often begins as an advisory layer running alongside existing dashboards, building credibility before it gains execution authority. The Governance Plane often emerges from the existing policy-as-code work a team has already done for compliance. The Execution Plane often begins as a small catalogue of high-confidence actions before it grows. The migration is real engineering work, but it is engineering on top of an existing stack rather than a green-field build. This incremental adoption is what the maturity model in section 4.9 makes explicit, level by level.

4.2 The Four Core Planes of Agentic Reliability
The architecture rests on four planes: the Signal Plane, the Reasoning Plane, the Execution Plane, and the Governance Plane. These planes are the structural grammar of an agentic reliability system, and every capability the rest of the book develops can be mapped to one or more of them. If you cannot place a capability cleanly into a plane, that is a signal that the design is incomplete or unsafe. We will return to this set so often across the rest of the book that it is worth slowing down here and getting the framing right.

The word planes is deliberate. Traditional architectures describe layers, and layers imply hierarchy. Upper layers consume lower layers. Failures propagate upward. The mental model is one of stacked dependencies. Agentic reliability needs a different mental model. Planes imply orthogonality. Each plane operates independently, but they intersect through well-defined interfaces. A failure in one plane should not automatically compromise the others, because each plane has its own job, its own contracts, and its own failure containment. A bad signal should not directly trigger execution. A reasoning error should not bypass governance. An execution failure should not corrupt intent. A governance rule should not require rewriting automation. Planes make these boundaries explicit and enforceable in code, not just in policy documents.

Before we develop each plane in its own section (4.3 through 4.6), it is worth understanding what each is responsible for at a glance, and what each must never do.

The Signal Plane is responsible for representing reality. It collects, normalises, enriches, and publishes telemetry in a form that machines can reason over. Metrics, logs, traces, events, topology, ownership metadata, and change context all live here, structured according to the decision-grade discipline of Chapter 2. The Signal Plane does not decide what matters. It does not infer causality. It does not trigger actions. Its job is to make truth observable. A Signal Plane that optimises for dashboards rather than machine consumption will limit everything above it, no matter how sophisticated the other planes become.

The Reasoning Plane is responsible for making sense of signals. It consumes signals from the Signal Plane, evaluates them against intent and policy, generates hypotheses, compares options, and produces candidate actions with associated confidence. The output of the Reasoning Plane is never a command; it is always a proposal. The internals of how reasoning works (the algorithms, the prompt patterns, the confidence scoring) belong to Chapter 9 and Chapter 10. What matters at the architecture level is the boundary: the Reasoning Plane reasons, and only reasons. If it can act directly, the architecture is unsafe by design.

The Execution Plane is responsible for changing the system. It performs actions that modify runtime behaviour: scaling, routing, throttling, restarting, failing over, degrading functionality. Execution is deterministic, bounded, and reversible. It does not decide what to do. It performs what has been approved. The plane is constructed around action contracts that declare what each action does, what preconditions must hold, what success looks like, and how the action reverses if it does not work. We will introduce action contracts formally in section 4.5; the property to internalise here is that the Execution Plane’s authority is structurally limited to what its contracts allow.

The Governance Plane is responsible for enforcing trust and accountability at runtime. It evaluates whether proposed actions comply with intent, policy, risk tolerance, and ownership boundaries. It decides what level of autonomy is permitted under current conditions. Crucially, the Governance Plane reasons about permission, not about state. The Reasoning Plane decides what action would be appropriate; the Governance Plane decides whether the agent is allowed to take it. This separation is the most counterintuitive of the four for engineers coming from a traditional SRE background, because it places governance inside the runtime rather than outside it as a review process. The Governance Plane’s job is to make the answer to “is this action permitted right now” something the system computes in milliseconds, not something it routes to a human for hours.

The planes interact in a continuous loop, not a linear flow. The Signal Plane produces signals continuously. The Reasoning Plane consumes them and produces proposals. The Governance Plane evaluates proposals and approves or rejects them. The Execution Plane performs approved actions and emits outcome signals back into the Signal Plane. The loop repeats. This is the DRAL loop explained inof Chapter 3 mapped onto an architectural structure: Detect is the Signal Plane’s job, Reason belongs to the Reasoning Plane, Act runs through the Execution Plane, and Learn closes the loop by feeding outcomes back to the Signal Plane. The Governance Plane sits between Reason and Act, enforcing the constraints that make Act safe. Every workflow in Part II is an instance of this loop applied to a specific operational concern.

The plane model is also a diagnostic. If your monitoring system triggers scripts directly, you have collapsed Signal and Execution; the architecture has no opportunity to reason. If your automation embeds policy logic, you have collapsed Execution and Governance; policy becomes invisible and unenforceable when the automation changes. If your AI system both decides and acts without constraints, you have collapsed Reasoning and Execution; the most dangerous collapse, because it removes the safety check that makes autonomy bounded. Each collapse increases speed and reduces safety. Each separation reduces risk and increases trust. Teams new to this architecture can use the four planes as a structural audit: where in our current stack does each plane live, where is each plane missing, and where are two planes collapsed into one? The audit usually surfaces work that needed to happen anyway.

One nuance about the planes worth flagging early. The planes are conceptual separations, not necessarily separate services or separate teams. A team may run all four planes through a single platform if that platform respects the structural boundaries between them. A team may also run them as four distinct services owned by different sub-teams, which is the pattern that scales best at larger organisations. What matters is the separation of responsibilities, not the separation of processes. A single process that produces signals, reasons about them, evaluates governance, and executes actions is implementing four planes only if the four responsibilities are structurally distinguishable inside it. If they are mashed together, the process is implementing one plane that does four things, and the safety properties the architecture promises do not hold.

The next four sections develop each plane in detail. The reader who has internalised the structure above can read the sections in any order; each plane stands on its own as a piece of architectural work. The reader who is encountering the framework for the first time will find the order matters: the planes build on each other, with each section assuming the conclusions of the one before it.

4.3 The Signal Plane: Designing for Machine Consumption
The Signal Plane is the foundation of the entire architecture. If it is poorly designed, every plane above it will either hesitate or act unsafely. No amount of reasoning sophistication or governance rigor can compensate for ambiguous, incomplete, or human-optimised telemetry. Chapter 2 developed the substrate this plane runs on (decision-grade telemetry, the topology graph, the Topology Agent, the five named observability failure modes). This section concerns how that substrate sits in the architecture and what its boundary with the rest of the system looks like.

The Signal Plane’s responsibilities are bounded and worth stating precisely. It collects telemetry across infrastructure, platforms, and services. It normalises signals into consistent schemas. It enriches signals with ownership, topology, and change context. It publishes a current, queryable view of system state. It maintains the agentic SLIs that measure its own quality (coverage, freshness, semantic completeness, schema conformance) so that the planes above it can know whether the signals they are reading are trustworthy. That is the whole job. The plane does not decide what matters. It does not infer causality. It does not trigger actions. Its job is to make truth observable in a form a machine can act on.

The boundary between Signal and Reasoning is where most teams go wrong. The temptation is to push interpretation into the Signal Plane, because doing so feels efficient. A signal that says “the system is degraded” is more compact than one that says “p99 latency is 412ms against a baseline of 38ms on a tier-1 endpoint.” The compact signal feels useful. It is also a category mistake. The moment the Signal Plane interprets, it is reasoning, and the Reasoning Plane above it is now reasoning on top of someone else’s reasoning, with no way to inspect the original evidence. The discipline is to keep interpretation out of the Signal Plane entirely. Signals describe state. Reasoning interprets state. Mixing the two collapses the boundary that makes the architecture safe.

The artefact that makes this boundary enforceable is the signal contract. A signal contract is a machine-readable declaration of what a signal represents, what guarantees the Signal Plane provides, and what decisions the signal is designed to support. A latency signal contract might declare its name, its version, its owner, the decisions it is intended to support (traffic shaping, scaling, rollback, escalation), its freshness guarantee (less than 30 seconds), its minimum observation window (5 minutes), its required confidence (≥ 0.75), the exclusion conditions under which it should not be trusted (maintenance windows, confirmed dependency outages), and the schema of the fields it carries. The contract is the interface. The Reasoning Plane reads against the contract, not against the raw stream. If the contract version changes, the Reasoning Plane knows. If the contract’s guarantees fail to hold, the signal is flagged and the Reasoning Plane downgrades its confidence accordingly.

This is what it means to design signals as a contract rather than as exhaust. Most observability stacks treat telemetry as exhaust: collect what flows, store it, query it, hope the right signal exists when you need it. The Signal Plane in an agentic architecture treats telemetry as a contract: each decision-grade signal is declared, owned, versioned, and bound to a specific reasoning use case. If a signal does not support any decision, it does not belong in the Signal Plane. (It may still exist for forensic purposes, but it sits outside the Signal Plane’s contract surface.) This is the move from coverage as a goal to coverage as a curated, deliberate property of the system.

The implementation detail worth flagging here is that signal contracts evolve, and the evolution must be managed with the same discipline as production code. Semver applies. Backward-incompatible changes require coordination with consumers. Schema validation runs at emission time, not at query time. The teams that get this right treat their signal contracts as a versioned API and their Reasoning Plane agents as the consumers of that API. The teams that don’t discover, six months in, that their agents are reasoning over signals whose meaning has drifted, with no way to detect the drift before it manifests as bad decisions. Chapter 2 named this failure mode silent decay. The Signal Plane is where it gets prevented.

Warning
A Signal Plane that optimises for dashboards will limit everything above it. The temptation to design signals for human consumption is constant, because the same engineering team usually maintains both the dashboards and the agents. The discipline is to recognise that the two consumers want different things, and to design the Signal Plane for the more demanding one. Dashboards can be generated from decision-grade signals; the reverse is not true.

The other property of the Signal Plane worth naming is that it includes the topology graph as a first-class artefact, not as metadata attached to other signals. The graph (developed in section 2.4) is its own queryable surface, maintained by the Topology Agent, versioned, owned, and validated against observed telemetry continuously. When the Reasoning Plane needs to know what a service depends on or who owns it, the Topology Agent is the API it calls. Treating topology as a first-class signal rather than as a wiki page is one of the largest single returns a team can make from agentic reliability investment, and it is the structural difference between an agent that reasons about blast radius and one that guesses.

4.4 The Reasoning Plane: From Signals to Decisions
The Reasoning Plane is where agentic reliability becomes visible. Signals alone do not improve reliability. Actions alone do not improve reliability. What matters is the quality of decisions made under uncertainty, time pressure, and incomplete information. The Reasoning Plane exists to transform structured truth into bounded, intent-aware decisions, and the architectural boundary that makes the plane safe is that it reasons but does not act.

The plane has four responsibilities, each of which we will encounter again in later chapters. First, it maintains situational awareness by continuously evaluating system state across time, topology, and change context. Second, it generates hypotheses about what is happening and why, including likely causes, affected components, and propagation paths. Third, it evaluates options by comparing candidate actions against intent, risk, and historical outcomes captured by the Learn beat of DRAL. Fourth, it produces decision proposals, not commands. Each proposal includes rationale, expected impact, confidence, and the authority level required to execute it. The proposal is the plane’s output, and the Governance Plane is what decides whether the proposal becomes action.

The output artefact deserves a name and a structure, because the rest of the book references it constantly. We will call it the candidate action. A candidate action is a structured recommendation that includes the triggering signals, the leading hypothesis, the candidate actions ranked by suitability, the intent alignment, the risk estimate, the confidence score, and the governance level required for execution. A candidate action might be represented something like this:

  proposal_id: "rp-48291"
  triggering_signal: "latency_anomaly:v1.2"
  leading_hypothesis: "dependency_saturation"
  candidate_actions:
	- "throttle_non_critical_traffic"
	- "scale_read_replicas"
  intent_alignment:
	primary_slo: "checkout_latency"
	allowed_degradation: true
  risk_estimate: "low"
  confidence: 0.82
  requires_human_approval: false
The candidate action is what crosses the boundary from Reasoning to Governance. The Governance Plane evaluates the proposal against policy without having to redo the reasoning, because the reasoning has been captured as a structured artefact. The candidate action is also what makes the system explainable to humans: when a human asks why the agent did what it did, the candidate action is the audit record. This is what Chapter 1 meant by saying that agentic systems must surface their reasoning rather than hiding it. The candidate action is where the surfacing happens.

The other deliberate property of the Reasoning Plane is that it reasons over contracts, not over raw data. The plane consumes signals through the Signal Plane’s contracts. It does not query the underlying telemetry directly. This matters because reasoning is only as good as the assumptions it can rely on, and the contracts provide guarantees the plane can take as inputs (freshness, statistical significance, exclusion conditions). Without those guarantees, every decision becomes a gamble. With them, the plane can reason with calibrated confidence, knowing exactly what its inputs are warranted to mean. This is the structural reason the boundary between Signal and Reasoning has to be sharp. Reasoning leaning across the boundary into raw telemetry would compromise the calibration that makes its outputs trustworthy.

Confidence is the property worth slowing down on, because it is the most often misunderstood aspect of agentic reasoning. A confident decision is not a correct decision. It is a decision whose assumptions are well-supported by evidence. The Reasoning Plane calculates confidence based on the confidence the signal contracts themselves declare, the consistency across multiple signals, historical success rates of similar reasoning patterns, the differentiation between competing hypotheses, and the completeness of the evidence. The output is a calibrated number that the Learn beat of DRAL will refine over time. The Calibration Error SLO (from section 3.6) measures whether that number is actually trustworthy. If the Reasoning Plane is producing well-calibrated confidence, CE stays low. If the plane is over-confident, CE rises and the Governance Plane downstream should respond by tightening the autonomy boundary until calibration recovers.

Low confidence is not a failure mode. It is information. A well-designed Reasoning Plane responds to low confidence by reducing the autonomy it requests in the proposal, triggering degraded modes, or routing the decision to a human via the patterns in section 4.7. The failure mode is unacknowledged uncertainty: a plane that produces confident-looking proposals when its evidence does not warrant the confidence. This is where Chapter 3’s confidently wrong signal becomes a structural concern. The Reasoning Plane is the place where confident-wrong-ness is either prevented (through honest calibration) or produced (through over-confident inference). Architecturally, what protects against confident wrongness is the discipline of treating confidence as a computed property of the evidence rather than as a vibe.

Several failure modes are common in early Reasoning Plane implementations. Overfitting occurs when the plane leans too heavily on recent incidents and ignores novel patterns. Under-specification occurs when reasoning lacks enough context and produces vague proposals the Governance Plane cannot evaluate. Action bias occurs when the plane prefers doing something over doing nothing, even when observation is the safer choice. These failure modes cannot be eliminated through tuning. They must be managed through architecture: explicit hypotheses, contract-bound signals, computed confidence, and the Governance Plane downstream. Chapter 9 develops the internals of how to design the Reasoning Plane to manage these risks; Chapter 10 develops the reasoning patterns themselves. What matters at the architectural level is the boundary the plane maintains: it reasons, and only reasons, and the proposals it produces are always subject to governance before they become action.

Warning
If the Reasoning Plane can act directly, the architecture is unsafe. The temptation to skip the Governance Plane in the interest of speed is constant, especially for routine actions. Resist it. The Governance Plane’s evaluation runs in milliseconds. The speed cost is negligible. The structural cost of removing it is that the architecture loses its ability to enforce that autonomy stays within accountability boundaries. Once the boundary is gone, it does not come back without rebuilding the system.

4.5 The Execution Plane: Acting Without Losing Control
The Execution Plane is where autonomy becomes real. Up to this point in the architecture, everything has been about understanding. The Signal Plane represents reality. The Reasoning Plane proposes decisions. The Governance Plane approves or rejects them. None of that changes the system. The Execution Plane is where intent becomes action, and it is also where most autonomy efforts fail when they fail. The common mistake is to treat execution as a simple continuation of reasoning. If a system can decide, surely it can act. In practice, unbounded execution is how reliability is destroyed faster than any outage ever could.

The plane has exactly one job: execute approved decisions exactly as intended, and nothing more. It translates decision proposals into concrete actions, enforces scope and authority and guardrails, executes safely and predictably, and emits outcome signals back into the Signal Plane to close the loop. It does not decide what to do. It does not reason about alternatives. It does not override governance. This separation is what makes autonomy survivable at scale.

The artefact that makes the plane’s behaviour predictable is the action contract. Just as signal contracts make the Signal Plane’s outputs trustworthy, action contracts make the Execution Plane’s behaviour bounded. An action contract is the machine-readable description of an action: what it does, what it requires, what it reverses, and what counts as success. The Execution Plane is constructed around a catalogue of action contracts, and the plane cannot execute anything not in the catalogue. Free-form action generation is explicitly outside the plane’s scope. This sounds restrictive, and it is. The restriction is the safety property. Reliability failures are rarely caused by lack of action. They are caused by unsafe action, and the lack-of-action problem is much easier to solve than the unsafe-action problem.

A concrete action contract for the “throttle non-critical paths” action introduced in Chapter 3 might look like this:

  action_contract:
	name: throttle_non_critical_paths
	version: "1.2"
	owner: team-platform
	intent: >
  	Reduce load on a degrading service by limiting non-critical request paths,
  	preserving capacity for high-criticality user journeys.
	preconditions:
  	triggering_signal: latency_anomaly:v1.2 or saturation_warning:v1.0
  	confidence_threshold: ">= 0.75"
  	service_criticality: tier-1
  	error_budget_remaining: "> 30%"
  	not_in_change_window: true
	action:
  	method: traffic_classifier_update
  	scope:
    	service: <from_signal>
    	path_classes: ["background", "secondary"]
    	throttle_percent: 40
  	duration_max: "10m"
	reversal:
  	method: traffic_classifier_restore
  	timeout_max: "30s"
  	automatic: true
  	trigger_conditions:
    	- signal: latency_anomaly cleared
    	- signal: error_budget_recovered
    	- manual: operator_request
	success_criteria:
  	primary: latency_p99_returned_to_baseline within 5m
  	secondary: error_rate_remained_below 1.0
	outcome_signals:
  	- latency_change
  	- error_rate_change
  	- throttled_request_count
  	- revenue_impact_estimate
Several properties of this contract are worth noticing. The intent field is a prose statement of what the action is for, addressed to humans who maintain the contract. The preconditions are machine-evaluable; if any one fails, the action does not execute. The action declares the scope mechanically (which paths, what percentage, for how long). The reversal is mandatory; an action that cannot be reversed automatically should rarely be in the catalogue. The success criteria are explicit and measurable, so that the Execution Plane can evaluate whether the action achieved its intended outcome. The outcome signals declare what the plane will emit back to the Signal Plane, which is what closes the DRAL loop and makes Learn possible.

The contract makes the Execution Plane’s behaviour predictable in a way that free-form action generation never can be. An agent in the Reasoning Plane can propose this action. The Governance Plane can evaluate whether the proposal complies with policy. The Execution Plane can execute it within strictly-defined bounds and roll it back automatically if it does not work. Everything about the action’s behaviour is declared in the contract, owned by a specific team, versioned, and inspectable. When a human asks why the system throttled traffic at 3am, the answer is reconstructable from the candidate action that triggered it and the action contract that bounded its execution.

EXAMPLE
The “throttle non-critical paths” action contract above is the canonical form. Every action in the Execution Plane’s catalogue follows this shape: intent, preconditions, action, reversal, success criteria, outcome signals. Teams adopting this discipline often find that the act of writing the contract clarifies what they actually want the action to do, what they actually consider success, and what they actually consider reversibility. The contract becomes the design conversation, not just the execution mechanism. Many teams end up with shorter action catalogues than they expected, because writing the contracts surfaces actions that nobody can describe completely enough to automate.

Blast radius is the property the Execution Plane enforces structurally rather than relying on the Reasoning Plane to bound. Every action contract declares its scope, its duration, its impact magnitude, and its reversibility constraints. The Execution Plane enforces these mechanically. Scaling a service by three replicas for two minutes is a fundamentally different action from scaling by fifty indefinitely, and the plane treats them as different actions with different contracts. This is not a policy concern, it is an architectural one. The Reasoning Plane proposes an action. The action carries its blast radius. The Execution Plane enforces it. No part of this depends on good intentions or careful operator judgement.

Two implementation patterns are worth flagging briefly because the rest of the book will encounter both. The first is centralised orchestrated execution, where a single execution service receives approved proposals, validates contracts, and performs actions through controlled integrations (cloud APIs, deployment systems, configuration management). This pattern offers strong consistency and centralised auditability, and it is the default for organisations with strong central platform teams. The second is distributed execution agents, where lightweight executors run close to the systems they manage, receive approved actions, and execute locally within strict contract bounds. This pattern reduces latency and improves resilience but requires stronger cryptographic controls and contract enforcement. Both patterns are valid. The architectural requirement is that execution authority remains structurally bounded, regardless of where the executors live.

Outcome reporting is the property that closes the loop. Execution is not complete until outcomes have been observed. The Execution Plane emits outcome signals back into the Signal Plane: latency change, error budget recovery, rollback invocation, action timeout, partial failure. Without these signals, autonomy cannot improve; it can only repeat itself. The signals feed the Learn beat of DRAL, which is where the system’s confidence in this action under these conditions gets refined for next time. An Execution Plane that does not emit outcome signals is producing actions but not learning, and a system that acts without learning is automation, not agentic reliability.

4.6 The Governance Plane: Encoding Trust and Accountability
The Governance Plane is what makes autonomy acceptable. Without governance, agentic systems are powerful but dangerous. With governance located in the wrong place (outside the runtime, in approval workflows, in CAB meetings), agentic systems become slow, brittle, and indistinguishable from manual operations. The architectural move that resolves this is to make governance a runtime concern. Policy lives in the system, evaluated automatically against every candidate action, in milliseconds, by the Governance Plane.

This is the most counterintuitive plane for SRE readers, because traditional operations places governance outside the runtime. Change advisory boards review changes. Approval workflows route requests for human sign-off. Runbooks encode policy as procedure. These mechanisms work when actions are rare and time pressure is low. They fail completely when actions are continuous and time pressure is high, which is the regime agentic systems operate in. The Governance Plane is the architectural response. It moves policy from a process outside the system into a runtime evaluator inside the system, and it does so without removing the human oversight that gives policy its meaning.

Governance does not reason about state. The Reasoning Plane already did that. The Governance Plane reasons about permission. Given this proposed action, with these inputs and this confidence, under these current conditions, with this team’s ownership and these compliance constraints, is the action allowed? The output is binary in the simple case (approved, rejected) and graded in the realistic case (approved at full scope, approved at reduced scope, approved with mandatory human notification, rejected with escalation). The plane consumes three inputs: the candidate action from the Reasoning Plane, the action contract from the Execution Plane’s catalogue, and the organisational policy that constrains both. Only when all three align does the action proceed.

A simple policy example clarifies what runtime governance looks like. Consider a policy governing autonomous scaling actions:

  policy:
	name: autonomous_scaling_policy
	version: "2.0"
	applies_to: scale_service
	allow_when:
  	confidence: ">= 0.80"
  	service_criticality: ["tier-2", "tier-3"]
  	time_window: "any"
  	error_budget_remaining: "> 20%"
	constraints:
  	max_instances_added: 3
  	max_duration: "10m"
  	max_cost_increase_percent: 5
	require_human_approval_when:
  	confidence: "< 0.80"
  	service_criticality: "tier-1"
  	affects_regional_failover: true
	escalation:
  	on_constraint_violation: require_human_approval
  	on_repeated_failure: tighten_autonomy
The policy encodes trust explicitly. It allows autonomy where risk is low (tier-2/tier-3 services, high error budget, conservative scope) and routes to humans where consequences are higher (tier-1 services, regional impact, low confidence). No meeting is required. No ticket is created. The system evaluates the policy at runtime against every proposal that matches its scope, and the answer is computed in milliseconds. The human cost of governance shifts from approving actions to designing policies, and the policy design happens on a slower cadence with more deliberate attention than approval workflows ever allowed.

This is what we mean when we say accountability does not delegate. Section 3.7 introduced the accountability boundary as the layer that surrounds the autonomy boundary. The Governance Plane is where the boundary is operational. The team that owns the service writes the policy. The Governance Plane enforces it at runtime. When the system acts, the team’s policy is what made the action permissible, and the team’s policy is what the system points at when the action is questioned. Accountability for the policy never moves. Execution of the actions the policy permits moves to the system. This separation is what makes agentic operations sustainable. Without it, the team either retains all execution (slow, manual) or abdicates accountability (fast, dangerous).

Authority levels are the structural concept the Governance Plane uses to make policy graduated rather than binary. Rather than asking whether an action is allowed or forbidden, the plane asks how much authority is appropriate. Common levels include observe only (the agent watches but does not propose), propose only (the agent proposes but cannot execute), execute reversible actions (the agent acts within tight scope with automatic reversal), execute bounded irreversible actions (the agent acts where reversal is impossible but the blast radius is contained), and require human approval (any action above the autonomy threshold routes to a human). As confidence rises and maturity improves, services can move up these levels. As confidence falls (per the Calibration Error SLO from section 3.6) or as conditions become unusual, services move down them. This is how autonomy scales without breaking trust: it is graduated, observable, and reversible at the level of authority itself.

Two implementation patterns are worth naming here. The central policy engine pattern places a single policy service in the runtime, evaluating every proposal against shared rules. This offers consistency and strong auditability and suits organisations with standardised governance requirements. The distributed policy evaluation pattern signs policy bundles cryptographically and evaluates them locally at execution agents. This reduces latency and improves resilience but requires careful versioning. The two patterns are not exclusive; many mature organisations use both, with the central engine for cross-cutting compliance constraints and distributed evaluation for team-specific operational policies. Tools exist in both shapes (OPA in the central pattern, signed bundles in the distributed pattern). What matters architecturally is that policy is evaluated automatically, deterministically, and at runtime, regardless of which pattern hosts the evaluation.

Auditability is the property that makes the Governance Plane trustworthy over time. Every approval, rejection, and escalation produces an audit event that captures the proposal evaluated, the policy applied, the decision made, the confidence at the time, and the accountable owner. Auditability is not for blame. It is for trust. When humans can review what the system did and why, autonomy expands. When the audit trail is incomplete or post-hoc, trust erodes and the team retreats to slower mechanisms. The plane’s audit output is also what makes regulatory engagement constructive rather than adversarial. A team that can show, for any past action, the candidate action that triggered it, the policy that approved it, and the outcome that resulted, is in a fundamentally different conversation with auditors than one whose answer to “why did the system do X” is “the agent decided to.”

Warning
If governance is external to the runtime, autonomy will eventually violate trust. Approval workflows and CAB processes work for low-frequency human-paced operations. They cannot keep up with continuous autonomous decisions. Teams that try to retain external governance while expanding autonomy will discover that the agent either waits indefinitely for approvals (collapsing into manual operations) or routes around them under pressure (violating the trust the policy was supposed to encode). The Governance Plane is the architectural fix. Policy moves into the runtime, evaluated by the system, in milliseconds, every time.

Dynamic guardrails are the extension of runtime governance that Chapter 11.2 will develop. Policies in the Governance Plane do not have to be static. They can adapt to current conditions: tighter constraints during high-traffic periods, looser constraints during low-risk windows, automatic narrowing of authority when Calibration Error rises, automatic widening when it falls. This is what allows the architecture to operate at L4 maturity (Adaptive, defined in section 4.9) rather than at L3 (Bounded). The Governance Plane is the surface where dynamic policy lives, and the discipline of designing dynamic policy is the work of Chapter 11. For now, the architectural point is that the plane is structurally capable of supporting it: policies are code, evaluated at runtime, observable through audit, and the same evaluator that handles static rules handles dynamic ones.

4.7 Human in the Loop as a Control Circuit
Human in the loop does not mean manual approval. That sentence is the whole argument of this section, and the rest of the section is the engineering work it implies. In an agentic reliability architecture, humans are not positioned as decision-makers for every action. They are positioned as control circuits that stabilise, calibrate, and evolve the system over time. Treating humans as approval gates collapses autonomy. Treating humans as absent removes accountability. Treating humans as control circuits preserves both, and this section is the canonical treatment that the rest of the book references back to.

The approval-based model breaks under agentic systems for three reasons worth naming explicitly. The first is speed. Agents reason and act at machine timescales; human approval introduces latency that defeats the purpose of autonomy. The second is volume. Agents generate far more decisions than humans can reasonably review in real time; the queue grows faster than humans can drain it. The third is quality. Humans reviewing partial context under time pressure often make worse decisions than well-constrained agents operating within defined boundaries. The combination of these three is what makes approval workflows structurally incompatible with continuous autonomous operation. The fix is not to refine the approval workflow. The fix is to relocate the human’s role.

The mental model that works is the control circuit from classical control theory. A control circuit does not decide every action. It observes system behaviour, compares it against desired outcomes, and adjusts parameters to keep the system stable. The agent operates continuously inside the loop. The human intervenes episodically, at specific insertion points where the human’s judgement adds the most value, and the agent continues operating between interventions. This shifts the conversation from “did the human approve this action” to “did the human design the conditions under which this kind of action would be approved.” The first question scales with the number of actions. The second scales with the number of action types, which is much smaller.

The architecture identifies three modes of human involvement, and the rest of the book uses these terms consistently. They are above the loop, in the loop, and on the loop. Each has its own job, its own appropriate cadence, and its own measurement.

Above the loop is where humans define intent. Service objectives. Acceptable risk. Blast radius tolerance. Cost constraints. Regulatory boundaries. The accountability boundary itself. These are encoded as policy and intent declarations, not as runbooks. Above-the-loop work happens on a slow cadence (weeks to months), with deliberate attention, in calm conditions, by the team that owns the service. The agent reads the intent at decision time and operates within it. The human is not present at execution; the human’s design choices are present in the policy that governs execution. This is the most valuable mode of human involvement in agentic systems, and it is the one most under-invested in by teams that frame the problem as “how do we approve agent actions.” The right framing is “how do we design the conditions under which the agent operates.”

In the loop is where humans participate in specific decisions in real time. Not every decision, by design; the discipline of the architecture is that in-the-loop participation is rare and structurally bounded by policy. When the Governance Plane routes a proposal to a human (because confidence is low, conditions are unusual, or policy explicitly requires it), the human enters the loop, reviews the candidate action with all its context attached, and approves, modifies, or rejects. The human’s experience in this mode is fundamentally different from traditional approval workflows: the agent has already done the investigation, structured the context, named the leading hypothesis, scored the alternatives, and presented the proposal as a coherent artefact. The human’s job is to bring judgement to a well-prepared question, not to assemble the question from raw materials. This is what makes in-the-loop participation high-value rather than draining.

On the loop is where humans observe ongoing autonomous operation and intervene only when something requires intervention. They watch the SLOs (the five flagships from section 3.6, especially Calibration Error). They review samples of autonomous decisions to verify they are well-formed. They adjust thresholds when calibration drifts. They expand or constrain autonomy based on what the trend evidence shows. This is the mode that scales: one engineer on the loop can oversee thousands of autonomous decisions a day, because the engineer is not approving them, they are watching the system that approves them. The SLOs are the engineer’s instruments. The audit trail is the engineer’s evidence. The policy is the engineer’s lever.

Note
The three modes: above, in, on the loop. Above-the-loop humans design intent and policy. In-the-loop humans participate in specific decisions. On-the-loop humans oversee operation and adjust calibration. Every team operating an agentic system distributes its human time across all three. Teams that under-invest in above-the-loop work find themselves swamped by in-the-loop decisions. Teams that under-invest in on-the-loop work find their calibration drifts and Calibration Error rises silently. The right balance varies by maturity level, and the maturity model in section 4.9 makes this explicit.

To make the modes concrete, consider an agent that automatically throttles traffic during suspected overload. The agent detects rising latency, evaluates risk, and proposes throttling with 85 percent confidence. Policy allows autonomous execution above 80 percent. The action executes. Later, the on-the-loop human reviews the outcome and notices that user impact was minimal but revenue loss was higher than expected (because the throttled paths happened to carry more revenue-generating traffic than the policy had anticipated). The human does not disable the agent. They update intent above the loop to include revenue sensitivity during peak hours, adjust the policy accordingly, and the next time the same conditions appear, the agent reasons differently. The human’s intervention was small, slow, deliberate, and architectural. The agent continued operating throughout. This is human-in-the-loop done correctly, and it scales in a way that approval workflows never can.

The other property worth naming is that human control should behave like a circuit breaker, not a kill switch. A circuit breaker trips when instability is detected, protects the system, and resets when conditions normalise. A kill switch stops everything, requires manual restart, and introduces fear and hesitation into routine operation. Agentic systems need circuit breakers. The human designs when breakers trip (specific Calibration Error thresholds, specific failure rates, specific anomalous conditions) and how recovery happens (automatic re-engagement when the SLOs recover, manual re-engagement when the trip was caused by an unrecognised condition). Kill switches still exist for extreme cases. They are the last resort, not the default safety mechanism.

This framing is what preserves accountability without collapsing autonomy. The owning team remains accountable for the intent definitions, the policy design, the threshold calibration, and the override decisions. The system acts on behalf of the team, within constraints the team defined, observed by the team’s on-the-loop engineer. The team is not less responsible than they were under traditional operations. They are responsible for different things. The shift is from approving actions to designing the conditions under which actions are allowed, and that shift is what scales reliability work to the velocity agentic systems make possible.

4.8 Reference Architecture: Putting It All Together
Everything described so far (signals, reasoning, execution, governance, and humans as control circuits) forms the structural grammar of an agentic reliability system. This section assembles those parts into a single operational reference architecture that a practitioner can reason about, adapt, and implement incrementally. This is not a vendor template. It is a conceptual build sheet, designed to be drawn on a whiteboard and adapted to whatever environment the team is working in. We will walk through it as a single worked scenario: a tier-1 latency degradation on the checkout service, handled by the architecture end-to-end.

At t=0, the checkout service starts to degrade. p99 latency on the `/api/v2/cart/submit` endpoint begins to drift upward. The Signal Plane is already observing the service through the latency_anomaly signal contract (introduced in section 4.3). At t=15s, the signal crosses the contract’s significance threshold. The contract emits a structured signal: service identity, endpoint, journey classification, criticality tier (tier-1), percentile values (p50/p99/p999), upstream dependency state (the Topology Agent reports that the payments dependency has been in a degraded state for the past three minutes), recent change context (a deploy landed on inventory-api 4 minutes ago), and freshness/confidence guarantees. The signal is decision-grade. Every property the Reasoning Plane will need is in the signal itself.

At t=18s, the Reasoning Plane receives the signal. It constructs the candidate action. It generates hypotheses: dependency saturation (high probability, given the payments-degraded signal), recent change (medium probability, given the inventory-api deploy four minutes ago), traffic shift (low probability, given current traffic patterns), capacity exhaustion (low probability, given current resource metrics). It scores each hypothesis against the available evidence. The leading hypothesis is dependency saturation, with confidence 0.82. It evaluates candidate actions against the leading hypothesis: throttle_non_critical_paths (high suitability for dependency saturation, low blast radius, fully reversible), scale_read_replicas (medium suitability, higher blast radius), rollback_inventory_deploy (low suitability for this leading hypothesis, but flagged as the contingency if the second hypothesis turns out to be correct). It produces a candidate action with throttle_non_critical_paths as the recommended action, intent_alignment confirming that allowed_degradation is permitted on tier-1 endpoints during incidents, risk_estimate low, confidence 0.82, requires_human_approval false (because the confidence exceeds the policy’s autonomous-execution threshold). The candidate action crosses into the Governance Plane.

At t=22s, the Governance Plane evaluates. It loads the autonomous_throttling_policy (the runtime policy governing actions of type throttle_non_critical_paths on tier-1 services). It checks the policy’s conditions: confidence ≥ 0.80 (passes, at 0.82), service criticality is tier-1 (handled by the policy explicitly, which allows tier-1 throttling under degraded-dependency conditions if the throttling preserves the high-criticality paths), error budget remaining > 30% (passes, currently at 67%), not_in_change_window (passes), the action contract’s max_duration is bounded at 10 minutes (within policy limits), the action contract’s reversal is automatic (required by policy). The policy approves the action with full autonomous authority, no human approval required. An audit event is emitted: proposal_id, policy_id, decision, confidence_at_evaluation, accountable_owner (team-platform). The candidate action transitions to the Execution Plane.

At t=23s, the Execution Plane loads the throttle_non_critical_paths action contract. It validates the preconditions one final time (signal still valid, confidence still above threshold, error budget still healthy). It executes the action: the traffic classifier on the checkout service is updated to throttle the secondary and background path classes by 40%. The action emits an action_started outcome signal back into the Signal Plane. The Signal Plane begins to observe the action’s effect. The Execution Plane has set a watchdog for the action’s success criteria: latency_p99 must return to baseline within 5 minutes, and the error rate must remain below 1.0 percent.

At t=180s, the watchdog reports. p99 latency has returned to baseline. Error rate has remained below 0.4 percent throughout. Throttled request count was 1,247 (small fraction of total). Revenue impact estimate is within policy tolerance. The action has succeeded. The Execution Plane emits outcome signals (latency_change: recovered, error_rate_change: stable, action_outcome: success). It begins automatic reversal at the 10-minute mark unless the underlying condition changes. The DRAL loop’s Learn beat picks up the outcome: the throttle_non_critical_paths strategy for this failure class (dependency saturation on checkout during payments degradation) is updated with a successful outcome. The Reasoning Plane’s confidence in this strategy for this failure class rises slightly. The next time a similar signal arrives, the Reasoning Plane will reach for this action with marginally higher confidence.

This is the whole architecture in motion. Less than four minutes from initial signal to confirmed recovery, no human in the loop for the routine execution, the on-the-loop engineer notified through the audit trail but not paged. Every step is observable, every step is reversible, every step is bounded by contract and policy. The audit trail captures the full reasoning chain. If anything goes wrong, the team can reconstruct what happened and why, down to the confidence score at the moment of decision.


Figure 6-3. The agentic reliability reference architecture. Four planes, one continuous loop, humans engaged at three points.
Now consider what happens when the scenario doesn’t go as planned. Suppose at t=22s, the Reasoning Plane’s confidence had been 0.74 instead of 0.82. The Governance Plane’s policy requires confidence ≥ 0.80 for autonomous execution on tier-1 services. The policy routes the proposal to a human instead. The on-call engineer’s interface receives the candidate action with full context attached: the leading hypothesis, the alternatives, the evidence, the recommended action, the recommended reversal, and the audit context showing why the decision was routed to them rather than executed. The engineer reviews. They have all the assembly work the agent has done already in front of them; they are not starting from zero. They approve the action with one click, and from that point the architecture behaves identically: Execution Plane runs the contract, watchdog observes, outcome flows back, learning happens. The human’s involvement took 30 seconds, and the engineer never had to assemble context from six different tools.

Now consider a worse case. Suppose at t=22s, the confidence is 0.82 but the policy detects that this would be the third throttling action on this service in the past hour. The policy’s escalation rule fires: repeated_action_threshold_exceeded. Rather than execute, the Governance Plane downgrades autonomy and routes to a human, with the audit trail explicitly showing the pattern of repeated actions. The engineer reviews. They see that the system has been throttling repeatedly without underlying recovery; this is the over-correction and hidden damage failure mode from section 3.8. They do not approve another throttle. They escalate to investigation. The architecture’s behaviour was correct: it recognised the failure mode through policy, surfaced it to the human, and did not let autonomous action mask the underlying problem. This is what it looks like when architecture catches its own failure modes before they become operational disasters.

EXAMPLE
The reference architecture in one figure: Signal Plane on the left producing decision-grade signals, Reasoning Plane consuming them and producing candidate actions, Governance Plane evaluating against policy and producing approve/reject/route-to-human decisions, Execution Plane executing approved actions through action contracts, outcomes flowing back to the Signal Plane to close the loop. The on-the-loop human watches the system through the audit trail and the SLOs. The in-the-loop human handles exceptions routed by policy. The above-the-loop human evolves the intent and policy that govern everything else. This figure is the picture the rest of the book references back to.

A few practical observations about how teams actually adopt this architecture. The first is that you do not adopt all four planes at once. Most teams start by strengthening the Signal Plane, because every other plane depends on it. With decision-grade signals in place, the Reasoning Plane can be introduced incrementally, often beginning in advisory mode (the agent proposes but does not execute). The Governance Plane becomes meaningful once the Reasoning Plane is producing proposals worth governing, which is usually when autonomous execution begins. The Execution Plane’s action catalogue grows over time, starting with the lowest-blast-radius actions (traffic shaping, feature degradation) and expanding as confidence and policy maturity allow. This incremental adoption is what the maturity model in section 4.9 makes explicit.

The second observation is that the boundaries between planes are where most of the engineering work lives. The signal contracts at the Signal-Reasoning boundary. The candidate-action format at the Reasoning-Governance boundary. The action contracts at the Reasoning/Governance-Execution boundary. The outcome signals at the Execution-Signal boundary. Each of these boundaries is an explicit, versioned, owned interface. The work of an agentic reliability team is largely the work of designing and evolving these interfaces, and the work of an agentic reliability platform team is largely the work of maintaining the infrastructure that hosts them.

The third observation is that the architecture is sketchable. A practitioner who has read this chapter should be able to draw the four planes, the boundaries between them, and the loop they form, on a whiteboard, in five minutes, without referring back to the book. That sketchability is the test of whether the architecture has been internalised. If you cannot draw it, you do not yet have it. If you can draw it, you can adapt it to your environment, and the rest of the book becomes a series of refinements on a shape you already understand.

4.9 The Agentic Reliability Maturity Model
Most maturity models fail practitioners for the same reason. They describe where you are, but they do not tell you what must be true to move forward safely. In agentic systems, that gap is dangerous. Autonomy is not unlocked by ambition or tooling. It is unlocked when specific capabilities exist across observability, reasoning, and authority, with governance enforced at runtime. Weakness in any one of those dimensions creates a trust ceiling: a level beyond which the system cannot operate safely, regardless of how advanced the other dimensions appear. This section installs the maturity model that the rest of the book references back to. It is the second-most-quoted section of this book after the Four Planes themselves, and it is the structural answer to the question “how do we know when we are ready for the next level.”

The model has five levels and three dimensions. The levels are Reactive, Augmented, Bounded, Adaptive, and Systemic. The dimensions are Observability (what the system can perceive and contextualise), Reasoning (how decisions are formed and validated), and Authority (what actions the agent is permitted to take). Governance is not a fourth dimension in this model because governance is the runtime mechanism that enforces authority; the level of governance maturity is implicit in the authority dimension. Each level is defined by what must be true on all three dimensions for the level to be safely operated. Skipping levels does not accelerate adoption; it produces the failure modes Chapter 3 named, especially confidence collapse (when authority races ahead of reasoning) and silent learning corruption (when reasoning races ahead of observability).

Level 1: Reactive (Human-Led)
Observability is siloed: metrics, logs, events, and traces live in separate tools, correlated manually by human expertise during incidents. Reasoning is static: if-then logic embedded in scripts or runbooks, with no situational awareness across components. Authority is human-only: humans acknowledge alerts and execute all changes. This is the baseline most organisations start from, and there is nothing wrong with it as a baseline. Agents introduced at this level do not produce returns; they produce surprises, because the substrate they need does not yet exist. The agentic SLOs from Chapter 3 are not applicable at this level. Traditional MTTR and availability metrics remain the right measurement.

Level 2: Augmented (Decision-Ready)
Observability is standardised: telemetry is tagged consistently, signals are linked to services and dependencies, and a meaningful fraction of telemetry is structured. Reasoning operates in advisory mode: agents propose actions, sometimes through ReAct-style patterns, but do not execute autonomously. Authority is suggestion-only: humans approve every write before it touches the system. Governance is documented policy with initial prompt-level guardrails. The primary metric at this level is the Suggestion Acceptance Rate (SAR): the percentage of agent suggestions that humans actually accept. A target of 80% or higher indicates that the agent’s reasoning is good enough to be worth listening to. Below 60%, the agent is producing noise; above 95%, the agent is so conservative that it is offering only what the human would have done anyway. SAR is the L2 metric and it is the gate that signals readiness for L3.

Level 3: Bounded (Policy-Locked)
Observability adds contextual narrative and causal-link awareness across services. The topology graph is queryable and validated. Reasoning becomes multi-step: the agent can decompose a problem, hold short-term session memory across the steps, and produce structured candidate actions. Authority allows autonomous execution for low-risk, pre-approved actions, gated by policy-as-code at the Execution Plane’s contracts. The Governance Plane is operational, evaluating proposals against policy at runtime. This is the first true autonomy threshold. The primary metrics at this level are Autonomous Resolution Rate (ARR) in the 20-40% range (most decisions still escalate, but a meaningful fraction execute autonomously) and Calibration Error (CE) below 25% (the agent’s stated confidence is well-aligned with its observed success rate). Action contracts are versioned. Reversal is automatic for autonomous actions. The on-the-loop role becomes the dominant mode of human engagement.

Level 4: Adaptive (Goal-Driven)
Observability supports ambient sensing of drift across services, often through knowledge graphs that compose topology, ownership, and historical patterns. Reasoning becomes reflective: the agent self-critiques its proposals, learns across incidents rather than within them, and adjusts its hypothesis generation based on the Learn beat of DRAL. Authority becomes dynamic: the agent selects tactics based on the current state of intent and policy, rather than from a fixed playbook. Governance becomes risk-adaptive: the Governance Plane’s policies are themselves parameterised by current SLO state, so that autonomy expands when calibration is high and contracts when it is not. ARR rises to the 40-70% range. Reasoning Latency (RL-SLO) becomes enforceable and visible. The system is now adapting rather than repeating, and the human’s role shifts further toward above-the-loop intent design.

Level 5: Systemic (Autonomous Ecosystem)
Observability covers the full semantic context of the system, including cross-tenant awareness and the relationships between agents themselves. Reasoning happens across collaborating agents: the Topology Agent, the Release Agent, the Business Impact Agent, the Resource Allocation Agent, and others coordinate through structured handoffs (Chapter 14 develops the coordination patterns). Authority extends to full-stack optimisation, including autonomous reconfiguration of services and policies within the constraints accountability boundaries impose. Governance includes non-repudiable audit ledgers, often cryptographically signed, so that the audit trail itself is structurally tamper-evident. ARR exceeds 70%. The L5 gate metric is Grounding Rate (GR) above 99%: the percentage of autonomous actions whose evidentiary chain (signal, hypothesis, policy, contract) can be reconstructed end-to-end. At this level, reliability is no longer reactive; it is self-maintaining within the constraints the team has declared. Very few organisations operate here in practice. The model includes the level to describe what it would look like if they did.

The five flagship SLOs from section 3.6 map onto the maturity levels as gates rather than targets. ARR rises as the maturity level rises. DQ-SLO rises with maturity (a more mature system makes better decisions when it acts). RL-SLO becomes enforceable only at L3 and meaningful at L4. AE-SLO rises with the quality of the action catalogue and the policy. CE is the one SLO that must remain low at every level; over-confident reasoning is dangerous at any maturity. Suggestion Acceptance Rate is the L2-specific gate. Grounding Rate is the L5-specific gate. The full set of agentic SLOs at a given level is the answer to the question “what does measurable readiness look like for this level.”

Note
The Trust Ceiling: the L2 → L3 transition is the hardest transition in the maturity model, not L4 → L5. This is the moment the system moves from agent-as-advisor to agent-as-executor, and it is where most adoption efforts fail. The failure mode is consistent: teams try to compensate for weak Governance Plane investment by adding stronger prompts to the agent, and prompt-level safety does not hold under load. The only sustainable path through the Trust Ceiling is structural: the Governance Plane has to be real, runtime-evaluated, and policy-driven before any autonomous write authority is granted. This insight will appear three more times across Parts II and IV. Internalise it now.

The Trust Ceiling deserves a deeper note because it shapes how teams should plan their adoption. The L2 → L3 transition is not a technical step that follows from natural improvement in the agent’s reasoning. It is a discrete organisational decision to grant the agent autonomous write authority for the first time, and that decision requires that the structural safety mechanisms (the Governance Plane, action contracts, automatic reversal, calibrated confidence) all be operational at the same moment. Many teams attempt the transition with three of the four mechanisms in place, and discover that the missing one is exactly where the first autonomous failure lands. The symmetry is unforgiving. A Reasoning Plane producing well-calibrated proposals does not compensate for a missing Governance Plane. An Execution Plane with sound action contracts does not compensate for unreversible actions slipping through the catalogue. A Signal Plane with strong agentic SLIs does not compensate for confidence models that have not been validated against outcomes. All four mechanisms are required, simultaneously, at L3. This is what makes the transition hard.

A few practical observations about how teams should use this model. The first is that maturity is not uniform across an organisation. A team operating a tier-1 service may be at L3 for that service while operating at L2 for tier-2 services and L1 for everything else. This is healthy. The model is meant to be applied per service or per workflow, not as an organisational scorecard. The second is that progression is not monotonic. A service may be operating at L3 and need to fall back to L2 temporarily because CE has risen above the L3 threshold. This is also healthy. The architecture’s ability to degrade autonomy gracefully is what makes the higher levels safe to operate at all. The third is that the model is descriptive of readiness, not prescriptive of pace. A team that is genuinely at L2 will not become L3-ready in a quarter by sheer ambition; the substrate work (signal contracts, action contracts, policy as code, agentic SLI instrumentation) takes the time it takes. A team that tries to skip steps will find itself in the failure modes of section 3.8.

The level boundaries are also where the architecture’s biggest investments tend to land. The L1 → L2 transition is mostly observability work: standardised tagging, signal schemas, ownership metadata. The L2 → L3 transition is the architectural transition: Governance Plane investment, action contracts, policy as code, the Reasoning Plane upgraded from advisory to bounded autonomous. The L3 → L4 transition is the learning transition: the Learn beat of DRAL becomes operational across services, calibration becomes adaptive, reasoning becomes reflective. The L4 → L5 transition is the coordination transition: multiple agents working together, cryptographic audit, full-stack optimisation. Each transition has its own characteristic work, and naming the transition explicitly is how a team allocates the right resources to the right phase.

The maturity model is also how the rest of the book stages its expectations. Chapter 5 on incident response assumes L3 or higher for the canonical scenarios; Chapter 11 on bounded autonomy assumes L4 for its dynamic guardrail discussion; Chapter 14 on multi-agent coordination assumes L5 for the most ambitious patterns. Readers can use the model to locate their own systems, identify which chapters describe their current operating mode, and which chapters describe the mode they are trying to grow into. This is what makes the model practitioner-ready rather than merely descriptive. It is a compass, not a scorecard.

4.10 Chapter Wrap
The architecture is the part of agentic reliability that determines whether everything else can be deployed. Without it, the workflows in Part II are aspirational. With it, they become engineering work. The four planes, the human-in-the-loop control circuit, and the maturity model are the structural inheritance every Part II chapter will draw on. Incident response in Chapter 5 will trace the DRAL loop through the planes for a canonical scenario. Delivery in Chapter 6 will treat deployment as a chain of action contracts evaluated by the Governance Plane. Chaos in Chapter 7 will treat BFI as a Reasoning Plane discipline executed through bounded action contracts. Pre-incident in Chapter 8 will operate the Signal Plane in predictive mode rather than reactive mode. Each workflow chapter assumes the architecture exists. The architecture, in turn, only matters if a team can sketch it, build it, and operate it at the maturity level appropriate to their context.

What this chapter has not claimed is that the architecture is finished work. The four planes are the structural grammar; the work of designing the specific contracts, policies, and agents for a given environment is what teams will spend most of their time on. The maturity model says when each level is safe to operate; the work of getting from one level to the next is mostly unglamorous infrastructure: schemas, contracts, policy as code, calibration instrumentation. The human-in-the-loop control circuit is the right framing; the work of designing the specific intent declarations and policy bundles for a given team is ongoing. None of this is plug-and-play. All of it is engineering, of the kind that compounds when done well and erodes when neglected.

Part I closes here. Three chapters in: Chapter 1 named why human-centred reliability cannot scale to where the industry is going. Chapter 2 named the observability substrate the next decade of reliability work runs on. Chapter 3 named the resilience loop that turns observation into learning, and the five flagship SLOs that make the loop measurable. This chapter has named the architecture that hosts the loop and the maturity model that paces its adoption. Together, these four chapters are the conceptual core of Agentic Reliability Engineering. Everything in Parts II, III, and IV will reference back to one of them. The reader who has internalised these four chapters can build, operate, and evolve agentic reliability in their own environment. The reader who has not will find the workflow chapters harder than they need to be, not because the workflows are difficult on their own, but because they assume a foundation that was meant to be laid here.

The next chapter is the first place where this architecture meets a familiar SRE workflow. Incident response is the most viscerally understood reliability practice in the industry, and it is where Agentic Reliability Engineering most obviously earns its keep. The test of whether the architecture has been internalised is whether the workflow chapter reads as a refinement on a shape you already understand, rather than as a fresh framework to absorb. If you can sketch the four planes, the DRAL loop, and the five SLOs without referring back, the workflow chapters that follow will compound on each other. If you cannot yet, this is the time to flip back and consolidate.
